Coffee Brewing Troubleshooting

Frequently asked questions about hand-brewed coffee and timers

Bitterness in coffee is usually caused by "over-extraction." This may be caused by the water temperature being too high, the grind scale being too fine resulting in too much fine powder, or the extraction time being too long. It is recommended that you try to adjust the grinding degree by one or two notches, or use our professional brewing timer to accurately control the water injection time of each stage to avoid the dissolution of burnt and bitter substances in the later stage. Explore Brewing Tools

The biggest enemies of coffee beans are oxygen, light, moisture and high temperature. The ideal way to store coffee beans is to place them in an opaque sealed jar or airtight bag with a one-way exhaust valve and place it in a cool, dry cupboard. Do not put coffee beans for daily use in the refrigerator to prevent condensation caused by temperature differences and accelerate the deterioration of the coffee beans.

Lightly roasted coffee beans have a harder texture and a more floral and fruity flavor, requiring higher water temperatures (approximately 91°C-94°C) and finer grinding for full extraction; dark-roasted coffee beans have a looser structure, deep caramelization, and easy release of bitterness, so it is recommended to use lower water temperatures (approximately 85°C-88°C) and coarser grinding. If you're not sure how to adjust, try our brew recipe calculator to create parameters tailored to different roast levels. Find tailored calculators and advanced guides for your specific coffee equipment.

The channel effect means that water flows out in the powder bed through the shortcut with the least resistance, causing some powder to be over-extracted and some not to be extracted, resulting in coffee that is sour and bitter at the same time. To avoid channeling, make sure the powder is evenly distributed, use a better-quality grinder to reduce fines, and keep the water flow steady and gentle when filling with water, without vigorously washing the powder bed.

The standard ground-to-water ratio for specialty hand-brewed coffee usually falls between 1:15 and 1:17 (i.e. 1 gram of ground coffee corresponds to 15 to 17 grams of water). 1:15 has a rich and mellow taste, while 1:17 is refreshing and bright. You can fine-tune it to your personal taste. With our Coffee Proportion Assistant, just enter the grams of coffee beans you have on hand and the system will automatically calculate the perfect amount of water for you. Explore Brewing Tools

The bed of coffee grounds expands during steaming because the beans release the carbon dioxide produced during roasting. If there is no expansion at all, the most common reason is that the coffee beans have been left for too long and have exhausted their exhaust (no longer fresh); in addition, if it is extremely light roasted coffee, the exhaust reaction will be relatively weak. Just make sure the beans are consumed within a month of roasting and they will usually retain good flavor.
