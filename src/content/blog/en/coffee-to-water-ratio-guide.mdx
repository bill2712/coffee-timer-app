## What is a brew ratio?

A brew ratio describes the weight of coffee relative to the weight of water. "1:16" means 16 g of water for every 1 g of coffee. For example, 15 g of coffee × 16 = 240 g of water.

It is the most basic brewing variable and also one of the most neglected. Many people spend hours on grind and temperature while measuring "about a scoop" and pouring "roughly that much", so strength changes from cup to cup and every other adjustment becomes impossible to compare.

**One sentence to remember: ratio mainly decides how strong the coffee is.**

## Why weight instead of volume?

- **Coffee**: a scoop's weight varies with grind, roast level and bean size. A scoop of light roast and a scoop of dark roast can weigh quite differently.
- **Water**: at room temperature, 1 ml of water weighs about 1 g, so weighing water on a scale is the simplest approach and captures what you actually pour.

A scale with 0.1 g resolution is the easiest way to keep your ratio consistent.

## How to calculate it

| You want | Calculation | Example (1:16) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water needed | Dose × ratio | 18 g × 16 = 288 g |
| Coffee needed | Water ÷ ratio | 300 g ÷ 16 = 18.75 g |
| Ratio of a brew | Water ÷ dose | 250 g ÷ 15 g = 1:16.7 |

Note that the ratio uses total water poured, not the final amount in the cup. Spent grounds typically hold about twice their weight in water, so 15 g coffee with 240 g water usually yields roughly 210 g of coffee.

## Common ranges by brewer

| Brewer | Common ratio | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pour-over (V60 etc.) | 1:15–1:17 | The most common home range |
| French press | 1:12–1:16 | Immersion; fuller body |
| AeroPress | 1:12–1:16 | The official everyday method is about 1:14 |
| Cold brew concentrate | 1:4–1:8 | Diluted with water or milk before drinking |
| Espresso | 1:1.5–1:3 | Very different strength and calculation |

These are common starting points, not rules. Barista Flow's recipe pages collect several sourced official recipes you can use as references.

## Ratio changes strength, not just "intensity"

Changing the ratio mainly changes the strength of the finished coffee, which you feel as body:

- **1:15**: stronger and fuller; flavours are more concentrated, and acidity and bitterness are more pronounced.
- **1:16**: a balanced starting point for many people.
- **1:17**: lighter and more delicate; good for larger cups or a tea-like style.

Ratio also affects extraction slightly, since more water can carry away a little more. But compared with grind and temperature, its effect on extraction is small. So:

- **Balanced but too weak** → change ratio (1:16 to 1:15).
- **Sour or bitter** → change grind first, not ratio.

A very common mistake is adding more coffee when a cup tastes weak. Strength goes up, but the original extraction problem remains.

## A strength-first adjustment process

1. **Start at 1:16** with everything else fixed.
2. Make sure the cup is balanced (not sour, not drying), adjusting grind if needed.
3. Once balanced, judge strength:
   - Too thin or watery → try 1:15.
   - Too heavy → try 1:17.
4. Changing ratio changes total water and may change contact time. If flow shifts noticeably, consider a small grind adjustment.
5. Record the ratio you like as the baseline for that coffee.

## What changes when you scale the dose?

Doubling 15 g to 30 g keeps the ratio but makes a deeper bed, longer pours and usually a longer total time. Many people find a larger batch needs a slightly coarser grind to keep a similar time and taste.

At very small doses (say 10 g) the bed is shallow and loses heat faster, so you may need a slightly finer grind or hotter water.

## FAQ

**What is the "golden ratio"?**
The SCA Golden Cup reference is often quoted as about 55 g of coffee per litre of water (roughly 1:18), while many home pour-over recipes use stronger ratios of 1:15–1:17. All of these are starting points, not the only correct answer.

**How do I calculate ratio for iced pour-over?**
Iced pour-over usually splits total water into hot water and ice, for example 15 g coffee, 150 g hot water and 100 g ice. You can use hot water plus ice as the total. With less hot water, you usually grind finer to compensate.

**Should coffee for milk be brewed stronger?**
Usually yes. Milk dilutes coffee flavour, so many people use 1:12–1:14, espresso or cold brew concentrate.
