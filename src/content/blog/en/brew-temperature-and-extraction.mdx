## Is "hotter means more bitter" true?

A common rule of thumb says hot water makes coffee bitter and cool water makes it sour. There is some truth to it, but it oversimplifies brewing.

Hotter water usually dissolves coffee compounds faster, so with everything else unchanged, raising the temperature speeds up extraction. However, research reported by the SCA found that when brew strength (TDS) and extraction are held at similar levels, the sensory differences between brew temperatures in the tested range shrink considerably.

In other words, temperature matters mainly because it changes how quickly and by what path you reach a given strength and extraction, not because a particular temperature carries a fixed flavour. That is why one person can brew a great cup at 85°C and another at 96°C.

## Kettle temperature is not slurry temperature

A temperature-controlled kettle shows the temperature of the water inside it. The water cools as it leaves the spout, travels through the air, lands on the grounds and touches the brewer. What actually drives extraction is the temperature of the coffee bed over the whole brew, which depends on:

- **Brewer material and preheating**: an unheated ceramic dripper absorbs a lot of heat; plastic holds heat better but still benefits from a rinse.
- **Pour timing**: the longer the gaps between pours, the more the bed cools.
- **Dose and water volume**: a 12 g brew cools faster than a 30 g brew.
- **Room temperature and drafts**: air conditioning or wind increases heat loss.
- **Altitude**: water boils at a lower temperature at altitude, so "just off the boil" is cooler.

So "I use 92°C" describes your operating condition. Record it to reproduce your process, not as a guarantee of flavour.

## Common reference ranges

A frequently cited range for filter brewing is roughly 90–96°C, and many pour-over recipes sit inside it. Roast level is often used to pick a starting point:

| Situation | Common start | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Light, dense roasts | 93–96°C | Harder to extract; need more energy |
| Medium roasts | 90–93°C | Typical pour-over starting point |
| Dark roasts | 85–90°C | Extract easily; lower heat reduces harshness and dryness |
| Official AeroPress everyday method | About 85°C | Immersion with pressure; a different brewer means a different start |

These are directions only. Variety, roast development, freshness and the flavour you want can all move the best temperature.

## A meaningful three-cup test

To learn how much temperature matters for your beans, run a small controlled comparison:

1. Use the same coffee and fix 15 g coffee, 250 g water, the same dripper and the same filter.
2. Choose three starting temperatures 3°C apart, such as 88, 91 and 94°C.
3. Keep grind, pouring rhythm and agitation identical, and record each total time.
4. Preheat the brewer the same way before every cup.
5. Once the cups cool to a similar drinking temperature, have someone shuffle them for a blind tasting.
6. Record acidity, sweetness, bitterness and finish, and note which cup you prefer.
7. If the cooler cup also flowed much faster or slower, admit the test has a second variable rather than blaming temperature alone.

### Reading the result

- **Little difference between the three**: your beans and recipe are not very temperature-sensitive; focus on grind or bean choice instead.
- **The hottest cup is clearly sweeter and more complete**: the coffee may need more extraction; fine-tune grind at the higher temperature next.
- **The cooler cup is cleaner and less bitter**: keep the lower temperature, or test a coarser grind at the original temperature and compare.

## Order of adjustments

Light roasts often need more extraction energy, but taste and flow should still guide you. If a cup is sharp and thin, adjust grind slightly first, then consider a higher temperature. If a dark roast is heavily bitter, try slightly cooler water or a coarser grind. Change one thing at a time so you know what worked.

A thermometer is more reliable than guessing how many minutes to wait after boiling. Without a variable-temperature kettle, Barista Flow's water temperature tool estimates cooling time from volume and room temperature, but it only provides a starting point.

## Avoid false precision

Spout shape, whether the lid is on, the amount of water in the kettle and airflow all change how fast water cools. Writing settings to a decimal place will not make coffee taste better. What matters more than "92.5°C" is:

- preheating the same way every time;
- starting to pour at a consistent moment;
- comparing blind rather than scoring while looking at numbers;
- repeating a test to make sure a difference is real.

## FAQ

**Can I brew pour-over without a temperature-controlled kettle?**
Yes. Take the kettle off the boil and leave the lid open for a short while. Measure a few times with an inexpensive thermometer and you will learn roughly how long your kettle takes to reach a given temperature.

**What happens if the water is too cool?**
Extraction slows, and the coffee may taste sharp and thin. If you brew cooler, you usually need a finer grind or longer contact time to compensate.

**Does temperature matter for cold brew?**
Cold brew compensates for low temperature with time, usually many hours of steeping. The principle is the same: the colder the water, the slower the extraction.
