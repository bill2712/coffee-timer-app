## Temperature changes speed, not flavor in isolation

Hotter water generally extracts coffee faster, but “hotter always tastes more bitter” is too simple. Research discussed by the Specialty Coffee Association found that when beverage strength (TDS) and extraction yield were similar, sensory differences across the tested brewing temperatures became much smaller. Temperature matters largely because it changes how quickly and easily a brew reaches a particular strength and extraction.

That does not make every temperature interchangeable. Heat retention, pour duration, room temperature, and coffee-bed depth all change the temperature at the grounds. The number displayed by a kettle is not the same as the slurry temperature throughout a brew.

## A useful three-cup experiment

1. Use one coffee, 15 g dose, 250 g water, and the same dripper.
2. Choose three starting temperatures separated by 3°C, such as 88, 91, and 94°C.
3. Keep grind, pouring pattern, and agitation unchanged, and record total brew time.
4. Taste blind at similar drinking temperatures; record acidity, sweetness, bitterness, and finish.
5. If flow also changes, acknowledge that the comparison now has another variable instead of attributing everything to temperature.

## A practical adjustment order

Light roasts often require more extraction energy, but taste and flow should still guide the decision. For a sharp, thin cup, test a slightly finer grind before a large temperature jump. For a heavy bitter dark roast, a modest temperature reduction or coarser grind may help—but change only one at a time.

A thermometer is more reliable than estimating a wait after boiling. Without one, a cooling-time calculator provides only a starting estimate.

## Avoid false precision

Kettle geometry, lid position, water volume, and airflow affect cooling. Record “92°C” as a reproducible operating condition, not a magic guarantee. Consistent measurement, blind tasting, and repeated trials matter more than adding decimal places.
