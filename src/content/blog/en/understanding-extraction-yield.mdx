## Why learn about extraction yield?

Extraction is the process of soluble compounds in ground coffee dissolving into water. Only part of a coffee bean can be dissolved; the rest is insoluble structure. Extraction yield (EY) describes what percentage of the original dry coffee ends up in the drink.

Learning about extraction yield is not about chasing numbers. It gives you a shared language: when you say a cup is "under-extracted", you know what you mean and which way to adjust.

## Separate TDS from extraction yield

| Measure | What it describes | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| TDS (total dissolved solids) | How strong the drink is | 1.30% means about 1.3 g dissolved per 100 g of coffee |
| Extraction yield | How much of the grounds was dissolved | 19.5% means about 3.9 g of 20 g grounds entered the drink |

They are related but not the same. Adding water to strong coffee lowers TDS but does not change extraction yield, because dissolved compounds do not return to the grounds. That is why "tastes weak" and "under-extracted" are two different problems.

## How to calculate extraction yield

A common approximation is:

**Extraction yield (%) = beverage weight (g) × TDS (%) ÷ dry coffee dose (g)**

### Worked example

- Coffee dose: 20 g
- Beverage weight: 300 g (weigh the coffee in the server, not the total water poured)
- Refractometer reading: 1.30%

Extraction yield = 300 × 1.30 ÷ 20 = **19.5%**

Total water poured cannot replace beverage weight. Brewing 20 g at 1:16 uses 320 g of water, but the spent grounds and filter usually retain roughly twice the dose in water, so the beverage weighs less. Using 320 g would overstate the result.

## The brewing control chart and 18–22%

The traditional brewing control chart plots TDS on the vertical axis and extraction yield on the horizontal axis, with an "ideal" box in the middle. That box is commonly cited as roughly 18–22% extraction and 1.15–1.35% TDS.

| Position | Meaning | Common sensations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Below the extraction range | Under-extracted | Sour, hollow, salty, short finish |
| Above the extraction range | Over-extracted | Bitter, drying, harsh |
| Below the TDS range | Too weak | Watery, faint |
| Above the TDS range | Too strong | Heavy, overpowering |

Keep in mind:

- The box comes from consumer research done decades ago; it is not a pass/fail taste line.
- Variety, roast, brewer and personal preference all shift the ideal.
- Some light roasts taste great above 22%; some coffees are fine at 18%.

The numbers are most useful for **repeating and comparing**, not for deciding whether you like a cup.

## What changes extraction yield?

| Adjustment | Usual effect | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Finer grind | More extraction, slower flow | Possible fines clogging |
| Hotter water | Faster dissolution | Preheating matters too |
| Longer contact time | More extraction | Also changes temperature and agitation |
| More agitation | Faster extraction | Can clog the filter with fines |
| Longer ratio (more water) | Slightly more extraction, clearly lower strength | Mainly a strength adjustment |

These interact, so change one at a time.

## Diagnosing without a refractometer

Refractometers are expensive and most home brewers do not need one. You can make the same decisions with taste and notes:

1. Fix dose, total water and brewer.
2. Record grind setting, temperature, cumulative water at each stage, total time and tasting notes.
3. If the cup is **sour, thin or salty**, increase extraction (grind finer first).
4. If it is **drying, bitter or rough on the finish**, reduce extraction (grind coarser or agitate less).
5. If it is balanced but **too weak or too strong**, change the ratio rather than the grind.

### What about "sour and bitter at once"?

This usually means uneven extraction: some grounds over-extracted and others under-extracted, so the average yield may look "normal". Changing grind may not help. Check pouring evenness, channelling and filter clogging first.

## Quality control when measuring

If you do own a refractometer, these practices reduce error:

- **Zero it** before every measurement with clean water, ideally the same water you brew with.
- **Sample well**: stir the whole server before sampling, not just the top or bottom.
- **Cool the sample** to the temperature your device requires, since temperature affects readings.
- **Filter if needed**: some brewers use syringe filters to remove particles, especially for immersion or metal-filter brews.
- **Repeat**: take two or three samples for important tests and check they agree.

If TDS barely changes but taste changes a lot, recheck pouring evenness and sample handling instead of forcing an explanation from extraction yield.

## FAQ

**Do I need to measure extraction yield for pour-over?**
No. It is a tool for understanding the principles. Most people brew excellent coffee with taste-based diagnosis and consistent notes.

**Is espresso extraction yield the same?**
The formula is the same, but espresso TDS is much higher (commonly around 8–12%) and its reference ranges differ, so do not apply the filter control chart to it.

**Does Barista Flow calculate extraction yield?**
Barista Flow does not connect to a refractometer or calculate measured extraction yield. The home page only calculates target water from dose and ratio. You can write your own TDS readings into your notes for later comparison.
