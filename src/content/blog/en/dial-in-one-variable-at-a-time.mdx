## Why dialling in often goes in circles

You open a new bag and the first cup is sour, so you grind finer, raise the temperature and brew twenty seconds longer. The second cup is bitter, so you undo everything. Three cups later the bag is lighter and you still cannot say which change actually helped.

The problem is not your palate; it is changing too many things at once. Brewing variables interact: grind changes flow, flow changes contact time, and contact time changes the temperature over the brew. Change two things together and you cannot tell which one caused the result.

This guide gives you a repeatable loop: diagnose, change one thing, then confirm with a record.

## Step 1: Separate extraction from strength

A weak cup is not automatically under-extracted; it may simply contain too much water for the dose. An intense cup is not necessarily well extracted. Before changing a recipe, ask two separate questions:

1. **Flavour direction**: sharp, hollow and short? Balanced and sweet? Or bitter, drying and rough on the finish?
2. **Strength and body**: too thin, about right, or too heavy?

The first question roughly maps to extraction and the second to strength. The Barista Hustle Coffee Compass uses the same two axes: increase or decrease extraction, and increase or decrease strength. It is a useful framework, but beans, roast style and water can create similar sensations, so treat every diagnosis as a hypothesis to test in the next cup.

## Step 2: Use taste to choose a direction

| What you taste | Likely direction | First thing to try |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sharp, short, salty, thin | Under-extracted | Grind one small step finer |
| Bitter, drying, rough finish, slow drawdown | Over-extracted or uneven flow | Grind one step coarser or agitate less |
| Clean but watery and faint | Strength too low | Less water (e.g. 1:16 → 1:15) |
| Heavy and intense but not bitter or drying | Strength too high | More water (e.g. 1:15 → 1:16) |
| Sour and drying in the same cup | Uneven flow or channelling | Check pouring and the coffee bed before touching grind |

What counts as "one small step" depends on the grinder. On a finely stepped grinder one click may barely matter; on a coarse-stepped one it may be significant. The point is to keep changes small and consistent.

## Step 3: Change one thing at a time

1. **Fix a baseline**: same coffee, dose, water, temperature, filter and pouring rhythm.
2. **Start with grind**: it has the most direct effect on extraction and is the easiest to undo. Sharp and thin → finer. Drying with a slow finish → coarser.
3. **Taste at a similar temperature**: very hot coffee hides acidity and sweetness, so wait until it is warm.
4. **Repeat once**: brew the same setting twice. If two identical recipes taste very different, the problem is pouring or preparation, not the recipe.
5. **Only then** test ratio or temperature.

### A sensible order

A common order is **grind → ratio → temperature → pouring technique**. Grind is easy to quantify and reverse. Ratio mainly controls strength. Temperature is harder to isolate because of brewer heat loss and pour timing. Pouring technique is the hardest to reproduce exactly.

## A worked example

Here is how a log can guide each decision (values are illustrative):

| Cup | Grind | Ratio | Temp | Total time | Notes | Next |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 6.0 | 1:16 | 92°C | 2:35 | Sharp, thin, short finish | Grind finer |
| 2 | 5.5 | 1:16 | 92°C | 2:55 | Softer acidity, sweetness appears | Repeat |
| 3 | 5.5 | 1:16 | 92°C | 2:58 | Similar to cup 2 | Try ratio for strength |
| 4 | 5.5 | 1:15 | 92°C | 2:50 | Fuller body, balanced | Keep as baseline |

Cup 3 changed nothing. It confirms that cup 2 was not luck, which makes every later decision more reliable.

## Common misdiagnoses

- **Treating good acidity as a defect.** Clear, fruit-like acidity supported by sweetness is different from a sharp, short, sometimes salty sensation. Light roasts are meant to have acidity.
- **Mistaking roast style for over-extraction.** Dark roasts naturally carry more bitterness; it is not always a sign of brewing too long.
- **Chasing a target time.** If a recipe says 3:00 and yours finishes at 2:40 but tastes balanced, keep it.
- **Sour and drying in the same cup.** This usually means uneven extraction: some grounds over-extracted, others under. Check for channelling, too much swirling or stirring, and a clogged filter.

## When to stop

When two cups in a row taste good and you can say why, stop. Dialling in is not about a perfect score; it is about finding a setting you enjoy and can repeat. With a solid baseline, a new bag usually takes one to three cups to dial in.

## Keep an actionable record

Five fields are enough: grind setting, ratio, water temperature, total time and overall score. Add one sentence stating what you will change next. That sentence turns tasting into action. ## FAQ

**Can I dial in without a scale?**
Roughly, but it will be hard to repeat. A scale is one of the most valuable tools for filter coffee because dose and water errors turn directly into strength errors.

**How many cups does a bag take?**
Usually one to three to find the direction. If you are past five and still confused, pause and check pouring consistency and the grinder.

**Why does a fresh bag change every day?**
Freshly roasted coffee is still degassing, which changes bloom and flow. Writing "day N after opening" in your log helps separate bean changes from technique changes.
