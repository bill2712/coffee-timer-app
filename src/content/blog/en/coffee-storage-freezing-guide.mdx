## Why does the same bag taste different two weeks later?

A bag that smells wonderful on day one can taste flat by week three. Roasted coffee does not "go off" like milk, but aromatic compounds evaporate and oxidise over time and the flavour gradually becomes less distinct. The goal of storage is not to stop change but to **slow it down and make it predictable**.

## The four main enemies

The National Coffee Association recommends opaque, airtight containers kept in a cool place, and buying in smaller amounts. That advice targets four factors that speed up change:

| Factor | Effect | What to do at home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oxygen | Oxidation weakens aroma and adds stale notes | Airtight container, squeeze out air, portion the beans |
| Moisture | Damp beans; mould in severe cases | Keep away from sinks and kettles; never open frozen beans while cold |
| Heat | Faster aroma loss | Away from stoves, ovens, windows and appliance vents |
| Light | Faster quality decline | Opaque containers or the original foil bag |

**Grinding** greatly increases surface area and speeds up aroma loss, so grind only what you need just before brewing.

"Best before" is not a safety date. Past its flavour peak, coffee usually just tastes less aromatic. If it has become damp, shows mould, smells musty or looks doubtful, do not drink it.

## Is the original bag good enough?

Many specialty coffee bags have a one-way valve and a zip seal. The valve lets carbon dioxide out while limiting outside air coming in. If you press out the air and close the zip each time, the original bag is usually fine for one to two weeks.

If the bag has no zip or is torn, move the beans into an airtight canister. Clear glass jars look nice but should be kept in a cupboard away from light.

## Room temperature, fridge or freezer?

| Method | Best for | Pros | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Airtight at room temperature | Coffee finished within one to two weeks | Simplest; no condensation risk | In hot climates, find the coolest spot |
| Refrigerator | Generally not recommended | Lower temperature | Humidity, frequent opening and food odours |
| Portioned freezing | Bulk purchases or special coffees | Slows change for longer periods | Must be airtight; avoid repeated thawing |

The problem with the fridge is humidity, frequent door opening and other food odours. If you want cold storage, portioned freezing is usually easier to control.

## A home freezing routine

1. **Portion by use**: for example 15–20 g per bag, or one to two days' worth. Use truly airtight bags or containers and press out as much air as possible.
2. **Label**: coffee name, roast date and freeze date.
3. **Store at the back of the freezer**, away from the door where temperature swings most.
4. **Let it warm before opening**: keep the sealed portion closed until it reaches room temperature so moisture does not condense on the beans.
5. **Use thawed portions quickly** and avoid refreezing.
6. **Compare instead of guessing**: brew a frozen portion and a room-temperature portion with the same recipe and record flow and taste.

### Extra care in humid weather

In very humid weather, the moment you open a cold container, water vapour condenses on the beans. That is why "warm first, open later" matters. Small portions only need a short time at room temperature; open them once they no longer feel cold.

Some people grind beans straight from the freezer. That can work, but cold beans may grind slightly differently. If you do it, do it consistently and note it in your log.

## Resting is a range, not a fixed date

Freshly roasted coffee releases a lot of carbon dioxide, and many people rest beans for a few days before brewing. Degassing speed varies with the roaster, packaging, roast level and brew method, so there is no single number that fits every coffee.

It is more useful to treat dates as observation points and record:

- days since opening;
- how much the bloom rises;
- total time and flow;
- how the taste changes.

If the coffee becomes easier to balance day by day, keep your current storage method. If aroma fades quickly, check your container and how you take beans out. Barista Flow's bean freshness tool tracks roast dates and suggested observation windows, but the cup is the final judge.

## A simple storage experiment

To find out whether freezing helps the coffee you usually drink:

1. On opening day, freeze half the bag in portions and keep the other half airtight at room temperature.
2. On day 7 and day 14, take one warmed frozen portion and one room-temperature portion.
3. Brew both with exactly the same recipe and taste them blind.
4. Record aroma, sweetness, clarity of acidity and finish.

If there is no clear difference, you may not need to freeze. If the frozen sample is clearly better, it is worth making a habit of it.

## FAQ

**How long do coffee beans last?**
It depends on roast level, packaging and storage. Many roasters suggest drinking within a few weeks of roasting for the best flavour. After that coffee is usually still drinkable, just less aromatic. Discard it if it is damp or smells musty.

**Can I freeze ground coffee?**
Yes, but ground coffee loses aroma faster and absorbs moisture and odours more easily. Without a grinder, buy small amounts and use them quickly.

**Why did flow change after freezing?**
Temperature affects grinding and extraction. Cold or freshly warmed beans may grind slightly differently. If flow changes noticeably, standardise the warming time before deciding whether to adjust grind.
