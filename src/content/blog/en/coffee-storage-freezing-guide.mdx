## The four main enemies

Roasted coffee aroma changes over time. At home, the most important exposures to control are oxygen, moisture, heat, and light. The National Coffee Association recommends an opaque airtight container in a cool location and buying smaller batches more frequently. Grinding dramatically increases surface area, so grind only what you need immediately before brewing.

A best-flavor period is not the same as a food-safety deadline. Beans past their sensory peak normally lose aroma rather than becoming unsafe. Coffee exposed to moisture, visible mold, or suspicious contamination should be discarded.

## Room temperature, refrigerator, or freezer?

For an amount you will finish within a week or two, airtight room-temperature storage is the simplest routine. Refrigeration can expose the coffee to humid air and food odors every time the container is opened. For longer storage, freezing in small sealed portions is easier to manage than repeatedly removing and returning one large bag.

## A practical freezing workflow

1. Divide the coffee into one-brew or several-brew portions in genuinely airtight packaging.
2. Label the coffee, roast date, and freezing date.
3. Let a sealed portion reach room temperature before opening it, reducing condensation on the cold beans.
4. Use a thawed portion promptly and avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles.
5. Compare fresh, room-temperature, and frozen samples using the same recipe rather than judging only by the aroma in the bag.

## Resting dates are not universal

Roaster, packaging, roast level, and brewing method all affect degassing. A date is an observation marker, not a promise. Track bloom behavior, flow, and flavor after opening. If the coffee becomes easier to balance over several days, keep the routine; if aroma drops rapidly, inspect the container and handling process first.
