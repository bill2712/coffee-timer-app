## Coffee beans are seeds

The coffee beans we buy are seeds from inside the coffee fruit. A ripe coffee fruit, often called a cherry, has roughly these layers from outside in: skin, pulp, a sticky mucilage layer, parchment, silver skin and finally the seed.

After harvest, producers have to remove or dry these outer layers and bring the seed's moisture down to a level suitable for storage and shipping. **Processing describes that journey.** It influences flavour, but it is not a flavour guarantee.

## The three most common processes

### Washed

1. Remove the skin and most of the pulp.
2. Ferment the seeds with their mucilage in tanks or dry, so the mucilage breaks down.
3. Wash off the remaining mucilage.
4. Dry the seeds in their parchment.

Washed coffees are often described as clear, bright and clean, because the fruit has less influence on the seed. Region, variety, fermentation time and roast still change the result a great deal.

### Natural

1. Dry the whole fruit, skin on, on patios or raised beds.
2. Turn it regularly and cover it when it rains.
3. Once dry, remove the dried skin and pulp in one step.

The National Coffee Association notes that uniform ripeness and careful drying are crucial to quality. Naturals are often described as fruity, sweet and full-bodied, but the word "natural" alone does not guarantee a particular fruit note. Poor handling can also bring over-fermented or earthy flavours.

### Honey / pulped natural

This sits between the two: remove the skin and pulp, but dry the seed with varying amounts of mucilage left on.

| Name | General meaning |
| --- | --- |
| White / yellow honey | Less mucilage, faster drying |
| Red honey | Moderate mucilage |
| Black honey | More mucilage, slower drying |

Producers do not use these names consistently. Sometimes they describe how much mucilage remains, sometimes how much shade is used while drying. Read the roaster's or producer's actual process notes rather than relying on the colour name.

## Comparing the three

| | Washed | Honey | Natural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fruit removed | Before drying | Before drying (some mucilage kept) | After drying |
| Water use | Higher | Lower | Lowest |
| Common descriptors | Clean, bright | Sweet, balanced | Fruity, full |
| Main risks | Fermentation control | Mould during drying | Uneven drying, over-fermentation |

The descriptors are tendencies only; individual lots vary widely.

## Other names you may see

Experimental processes have become more common, for example:

- **Anaerobic fermentation**: fermented in sealed, low-oxygen conditions; flavours are often intense and distinctive.
- **Carbonic maceration**: borrowed from winemaking; fermented in containers filled with carbon dioxide.
- **Wet-hulled (Giling Basah)**: common in Indonesia; parchment is removed while the seed is still fairly wet, often giving herbal or earthy notes.

Methods behind these labels vary a lot, so the roaster's explanation is the best guide.

## Turning processing into a brewing test

1. **Start with your baseline recipe**: same ratio and temperature, so you do not assume a result from the label.
2. **Intensely fruity naturals or anaerobics**: if flavours feel heavy or ferment-forward, reduce agitation first and see whether clarity and finish improve.
3. **Washed coffee that tastes sharp and thin**: grind one small step finer before making a big temperature drop.
4. **Honey process**: usually start from your baseline and adjust by taste.
5. **Record what you actually taste**: do not treat processing as a fixed flavour category.

## Reading the bag label

A detailed label often includes:

| Information | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| Country / region / farm | Terroir and growing conditions |
| Variety | For example Typica, Bourbon or Geisha; flavour potential |
| Altitude | Higher altitudes are often associated with denser beans |
| Process | The topic of this article |
| Harvest | Whether the green coffee is fresh |
| Roast date | Roasted coffee condition and resting time |
| Tasting notes | The roaster's impressions, for reference |

A label that only says "natural" gives you a rough starting point at best. Your own brew log is what tells you whether a process behaves consistently across different coffees.

## A simple comparison exercise

To taste processing differences yourself:

1. Find a washed and a natural coffee from the same region, ideally the same farm. Many roasters sell them as sets.
2. Brew both with exactly the same recipe.
3. Taste side by side once cooled and note aroma, acidity, sweetness, body and finish.

This kind of single-variable comparison builds your palate faster than any article.

## FAQ

**Which process is best?**
None; it depends on your taste. People who love clarity and brightness may prefer washed coffees, while those who love fruit and body may prefer naturals.

**Are naturals more likely to have defects?**
Natural processing demands careful drying, and poor handling can cause over-fermentation. Well-made naturals can be very clean.

**Does processing change how I should store beans?**
Storage is largely the same for all roasted coffee: avoid oxygen, moisture, heat and light. Processing mainly affects flavour, not storage.
