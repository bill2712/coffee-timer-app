## Two basic ways to extract

Home coffee brewers look endlessly varied, but most rely on one of two principles:

- **Percolation**: water flows continuously through a bed of coffee, carrying dissolved compounds out. Pour-over drippers work this way.
- **Immersion**: grounds sit fully submerged in water for a period, then the liquid is separated. The French press works this way.

The AeroPress is mainly immersion, finished by pushing the coffee through a filter with pressure, so it shares traits of both.

Understanding this explains why grind, time and body differ so much between brewers.

## The three at a glance

| | Pour-over (e.g. V60) | French press | AeroPress |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Principle | Percolation | Immersion | Immersion + pressure filtration |
| Filter | Paper | Metal mesh | Paper (metal discs also available) |
| Body | Clean and clear | Full, with oils and some fines | In between, depending on filter |
| Grind | Medium-fine | Coarse | Medium-fine to fine |
| Time | About 2:30–3:30 | About 4-minute steep | About 1–2 minutes |
| Technique demand | Higher | Low | Low to medium |
| Batch size | One or two cups | One to many cups | Mainly one cup |
| Cleaning | Easy (discard the filter) | More work (empty grounds, wash mesh) | Easy (eject the puck) |
| Portability | Average | Poor | Very good |

## Pour-over drippers

### A good fit if you…

- enjoy clean, clear flavours and want to taste the florals and fruit in light roasts;
- like the brewing ritual and are willing to practise pouring;
- want to understand how grind, temperature and pouring affect taste.

### Things to know

Pour-over has the most variables: pour speed, position, height, stages and agitation all matter. Beginners may see bigger cup-to-cup differences at first, but it is also the brewer that teaches you the most.

**Starting recipe**: James Hoffmann's one-cup V60 technique uses 15 g coffee and 250 g water in five equal pours, finishing around 3:00. See our recipe pages for details.

## French press

### A good fit if you…

- like a full, rounded body;
- want to brew several cups at once, for example for a family breakfast;
- do not want to practise pouring technique.

### Things to know

- The metal mesh lets some fines and oils into the cup, giving more body but a little sediment at the bottom.
- **Pour the coffee out immediately** after steeping, or it keeps extracting and becomes bitter and drying.
- Grinding too fine makes plunging hard and adds more fines to the cup.

**Starting recipes**: Stumptown's method uses 56 g of coarse coffee with 850 g of water, steeped for four minutes, then pressed and decanted immediately. For a small press, Blue Bottle's 30 g with 350 g of water is a useful reference.

## AeroPress

### A good fit if you…

- want a quick, consistent single cup;
- travel often or brew at the office;
- enjoy experimenting with recipes (the AeroPress has endless variations).

### Things to know

- It usually makes one cup at a time.
- Press steadily and gently; pressing too hard can compress the grounds or even cause leaks.
- Standard and inverted methods are both common. If you use the inverted method, flip carefully to avoid burns.

**Starting recipe**: the official everyday AeroPress method uses about 16–18 g of medium-fine coffee, water at about 85°C, a short stir and a gentle press.

## Where body differences come from

| Factor | Pour-over | French press | AeroPress |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Coffee oils | Mostly absorbed by paper | Mostly retained | Depends on filter |
| Fines | Mostly held by paper | Some reach the cup | Fewer with paper |
| Contact | Fresh water keeps flowing through | The same water steeps throughout | Steep, then brief pressure |

This explains why the same coffee can taste clear and bright as pour-over yet rounder and heavier from a French press. Neither is wrong; they simply present the coffee differently.

## Choosing for your routine

| Your situation | Consider |
| --- | --- |
| Rushed mornings, one cup | AeroPress or a flat-bottom dripper |
| Slow weekends, enjoying the ritual | Conical pour-over dripper |
| Coffee for the whole household | French press |
| Frequent travel or camping | AeroPress |
| Wanting to learn extraction | Pour-over |
| Adding milk | French press or a stronger AeroPress |

## Same coffee, three brewers: a comparison exercise

If you own two or three of these brewers, try this:

1. Use the same medium-roast coffee.
2. Brew with each brewer's starting recipe, adjusting grind for each.
3. Taste side by side once cooled and note body, acidity, sweetness and finish.

You will quickly see that the brewer itself is a variable and learn which body you prefer.

## FAQ

**Can I use a pour-over grind in a French press?**
You can try, but expect more fines, harder plunging and more sediment. A coarser grind is generally recommended.

**Which brewer is best for cold brew?**
Cold brew is an immersion method, so a French press or a dedicated cold brew jar both work. See our cold brew calculator and sourced cold brew concentrate recipe.

**If I can only buy one, which should it be?**
If you are unsure, the AeroPress or French press is more forgiving. If you want to dig deep, a pour-over dripper teaches you the most.
