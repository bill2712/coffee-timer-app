## Why learn to taste?

Every brewing adjustment is ultimately judged by taste. If all you can say is "good" or "not good", it is hard to know what to change next.

Tasting does not require a naturally sensitive tongue. It is more like a descriptive skill: once you have some basic vocabulary and a way to compare, vague impressions become information you can act on.

## Basic tasting steps

1. **Smell first**: right after brewing, smell the coffee and note your first impression, such as floral, fruity, nutty or chocolatey.
2. **Let it cool**: many flavours are hidden when coffee is very hot. Take the first proper sip once it is warm.
3. **Slurp**: sip from a spoon or the cup so the coffee spreads across your mouth with some air. Noise is fine; it helps aromas reach your nose.
4. **Evaluate separately**: notice acidity, sweetness, bitterness, body and finish one at a time.
5. **Taste again**: flavour changes as coffee cools. Many coffees are sweetest when warm, and acidity often becomes clearer as they cool.

## Five dimensions to describe

| Dimension | Ask yourself | Common words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acidity | Bright or sharp? Strong or soft? | Citrus, berry, apple, gentle, sharp |
| Sweetness | Is it there? What is it like? | Caramel, honey, brown sugar, fruity sweetness |
| Bitterness | Rounded or harsh? | Cocoa, dark chocolate, burnt |
| Body | Thin or full? Smooth or rough? | Tea-like, silky, heavy, drying |
| Finish | How long? Pleasant? | Clean, long, sweet, drying |

You do not need to fill every field every time. At first, being able to say whether the acidity is pleasant, whether there is sweetness and whether the body is thin or full is enough to guide most adjustments.

## The flavour wheel is a reference, not a test

The Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel, developed by the SCA with World Coffee Research, arranges common flavours from broad categories in the centre to specific descriptors on the outside. It is an excellent vocabulary list, but beginners do not need to force themselves to find "lychee" or "jasmine".

A practical approach is to start with the broad inner categories, such as "fruity", "nutty/cocoa" or "floral", and then narrow outwards, for example fruity → berry → blueberry. Saying "it has a berry quality" is already a useful description.

## Blind tasting removes expectations

We are easily influenced by labels: knowing a coffee was expensive or a grinder is new makes us think it tastes better. Blind tasting reduces that bias:

1. Prepare two cups that differ in only one variable, such as grind.
2. Ask someone to mark the bottom of each cup and shuffle them.
3. Taste, take notes and choose the one you prefer.
4. Reveal the answer afterwards.

If you pick the same cup several times in a row, the difference is real. If your choices look random, that variable matters little to you.

## A five-field log: simple but sufficient

Long reflections are hard to compare. These five fields support most decisions:

| Field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Grind setting | 5.5 |
| Ratio | 1:16 |
| Water temperature | 92°C |
| Total time | 2:55 |
| Score (1–5) | 4 |

Then add two sentences:

- **One taste description**: "Soft acidity, caramel sweetness, slightly drying finish."
- **One next step**: "Next cup: grind half a step coarser."

The next-step sentence matters most. It turns tasting into action and tells you exactly what to do when you open your log next time.

## Keep scoring consistent

Scores are for comparison, not absolute quality. A simple 1–5 scale works:

| Score | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Clearly flawed; would not drink again |
| 2 | Drinkable with obvious faults |
| 3 | Balanced, no major issues |
| 4 | Good, with clear strengths |
| 5 | Excellent; worth remembering this setting |

Scores are most meaningful within the same coffee. Between different coffees, style and personal preference affect the numbers.

## Common pitfalls

- **Scoring while hot**: acidity and sweetness are muted, so cups get underrated.
- **Only one sip**: coffee changes as it cools; taste at least at two temperatures.
- **Too many cups in a row**: your palate tires. Take small sips and drink water between cups.
- **Environmental effects**: strong food or toothpaste changes perception.
- **Over-reaching for vocabulary**: rather than forcing "bergamot", honestly writing "citrusy, a bit bitter" is more useful.

## Turning notes into progress

After a dozen or so entries, look back:

- Do your highest-scoring cups share a grind or ratio?
- Do certain coffees always need hotter water?
- Do you prefer stronger or lighter coffee?

These patterns become your personal baseline. Starting a new bag from your baseline rather than a generic online recipe usually finds a good cup faster.

## FAQ

**Do I need professional cupping?**
No. Cupping is a standardised way to evaluate many samples at once. At home, a consistent method is enough to compare effectively.

**I cannot taste the notes on the bag. Is that my fault?**
No. Flavour descriptions depend on personal experience and culture, and a roaster's notes reflect their impressions. What matters is that you can tell two cups apart.
