## First ask: which equipment affects taste most?

When you start brewing pour-over, it is easy to be drawn to attractive drippers, temperature-controlled kettles and all kinds of servers. Yet the items with the biggest effect on flavour are often the least glamorous.

This guide ranks equipment by its effect on the cup and explains what problem each item solves. We do not recommend specific brands or models and have no affiliate links; choose according to your budget and habits.

## Priority overview

| Order | Item | Problem it solves | Temporary substitute? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Fresh beans | The source of flavour | No |
| 2 | Scale | Consistent dose and water | Hard |
| 3 | Burr grinder | Consistent, adjustable grind | A café can grind for you short term |
| 4 | Dripper and filters | The water's flow path | No |
| 5 | Pour-over kettle | Control of stream size and position | Yes, with less control |
| 6 | Thermometer or variable-temperature kettle | Consistent water temperature | Estimate by waiting time |
| 7 | Server, timer, etc. | Convenience | Use a mug and your phone |

## 1. Fresh beans

The best equipment cannot rescue stale or poor-quality beans. When starting out:

- Buy coffee with a **roast date**, not just a best-before date.
- Buy small bags (for example 100–250 g) and finish them within two or three weeks.
- Buy whole beans and grind just before brewing.

## 2. A scale

Many people put the grinder first, but for beginners a scale often gives a more immediate improvement. Without one:

- each scoop weighs differently, so strength changes every cup;
- you do not know how much water you have poured, so staged pouring is impossible;
- no adjustment can be compared, because the baseline keeps moving.

**What to look for**: 0.1 g resolution, a capacity of at least 1–2 kg and a quick response. Coffee scales with built-in timers are convenient, but a kitchen scale and phone timer work too.

## 3. A burr grinder

Grind consistency directly affects how evenly coffee extracts.

| Type | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hand burr grinder | Relatively affordable, compact, quiet | Takes effort each time |
| Electric burr grinder | Convenient and fast | More expensive and larger |
| Blade grinder | Cheap | Very uneven particle sizes; hard to control |
| Pre-ground coffee | No equipment needed | Loses aroma quickly; grind cannot be adjusted |

**What to look for**: fine adjustment steps, stable output and easy cleaning.

**Temporary substitute**: before buying a grinder, ask a café to grind for pour-over, buy small amounts and use them quickly.

## 4. Dripper and filters

Common drippers have different designs:

| Type | Characteristics |
| --- | --- |
| Conical (e.g. V60) | One large opening; flow depends mostly on grind and pouring; flexible but technique-sensitive |
| Flat-bottom | A more even bed; more forgiving of pouring technique |
| Immersion-drip hybrid | Steep first, then release; simple and consistent |

There is no single best choice for beginners. Conical drippers have the most recipes and information; flat-bottom drippers make consistent results easier.

**Materials**: plastic holds heat well, is durable and inexpensive; ceramic and glass look good but need thorough preheating; metal conducts heat quickly and lasts a long time.

**Filters**: use filters that match your dripper size. Switching brands can change flow noticeably, so test a new filter as a new variable.

## 5. A pour-over kettle

A gooseneck kettle lets you control the thickness, height and position of the stream. A regular kettle produces a thick, fast stream with more agitation, making consistent staged pouring harder.

**What to look for**: a long, narrow spout, a steady stream and enough capacity (usually 600 ml–1 L).

**Temporary substitute**: with a regular kettle, pour lower, slower and consistently. Results may not be perfect, but you can still make good coffee.

## 6. A thermometer or variable-temperature kettle

A temperature-controlled kettle is convenient but not essential. An inexpensive food thermometer and a few measurements will teach you how long your kettle takes to cool to a given temperature.

Barista Flow's water temperature tool estimates cooling time from volume and room temperature as a starting point if you do not have a variable-temperature kettle.

## 7. Other accessories

- **Server**: brewing straight into a mug works, as long as it fits on the scale.
- **Timer**: a phone is enough; Barista Flow's timer also shows each pour target.
- **Filter holders, dosing cups, brushes**: convenient but do not affect taste.

## A suggested starter set on a budget

If you can only buy a few items first, consider:

1. A scale (0.1 g)
2. An entry-level hand burr grinder
3. A plastic conical or flat-bottom dripper with matching filters

The kettle and thermometer can come later. This set already lets you repeat a recipe reliably and start making meaningful adjustments.

## Common buying mistakes

- **Buying the most expensive dripper first**: differences between drippers are much smaller than differences in grinding and dose control.
- **Pairing a blade grinder with expensive beans**: uneven grinding wastes the beans' potential.
- **Buying too much coffee at once**: unless you portion and freeze it, aroma fades before you finish.
- **Choosing by looks alone**: glass and ceramic need more preheating; if you are often in a hurry, plastic may be more practical.

## FAQ

**Do I have to use a V60?**
No. The V60 is popular and has many recipes, but any dripper can make great coffee. Barista Flow's equipment chooser can suggest options based on your habits.

**Are French presses or AeroPresses good for beginners?**
Yes. Immersion brewers are less sensitive to pouring technique and give consistent results more easily. See our brewer comparison article.

**How should I clean my equipment?**
Rinse with hot water after each use and periodically wash with a mild detergent to remove coffee oils. Clean grinders as the manufacturer instructs, and do not rinse burrs with water unless the instructions allow it.
