## A grind setting describes only part of the picture

Many people think of grind as a single number: "I grind at 20 clicks" or "this recipe uses medium-fine". In reality, a grinder setting mainly tells you which way the bulk of the particles moves.

Ground coffee always contains a range: a main population of particles, some larger pieces (often called boulders) and some very small particles (fines). That range is the **particle size distribution**. Two grinders set to the same average size and producing the same brew time can still taste different because the shape of their distributions differs.

"Uniform" does not mean every particle is identical. Every grinder produces a distribution. The useful question is whether that distribution gives stable flow and clear flavour with your brewer and recipe.

## Three parts of the distribution

| Part | Characteristics | Effect on brewing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fines | Large surface area; extract quickly | Can migrate to the filter and slow flow; too many can add dryness |
| Main particles | Set the overall grind size | Drive extraction speed and total time |
| Boulders | Interior takes longer to reach | Too many can make the cup thin or sharp |

Fines are not simply "bad". Some fines contribute to body and are an unavoidable result of grinding. Problems arise when there are too many fines or too many boulders, so that one cup contains both under- and over-extracted coffee.

## Burr grinders versus blade grinders

### Burr grinders

A burr grinder crushes beans between two burrs, and particles leave only when they are small enough to pass the gap, so size is easier to control. Flat and conical burrs produce differently shaped distributions, but both can grind consistently and repeatably.

### Blade grinders

A blade grinder smashes beans with a spinning blade; the longer it runs, the finer the result. The problem is a very wide spread: some chunks remain while other coffee turns to powder. That makes flow and extraction hard to control.

If you use a blade grinder and often get cups that are both sour and drying, consider the limitation of the equipment before endlessly changing the recipe.

## Slow flow is not always caused by fines

Filter seating, pour height, swirling, degassing and water all affect flow. Judging a grinder by total time alone often leads to the wrong conclusion. For example:

- very fresh coffee releases lots of gas, which can hold up water during the bloom;
- excessive swirling or stirring pushes fines toward the filter;
- a filter sealed against the outlet slows drainage.

Rule out pouring and filter issues before blaming the grinder.

## A repeatable comparison

To see how much a grind change affects your brew:

1. Use the same coffee, dose, water and temperature.
2. Keep the pouring pattern and timing identical; change only the grind setting.
3. Record end of bloom, end of main pour and end of drawdown.
4. Once cups cool to a similar temperature, note acidity, sweetness, dryness and finish.
5. Repeat at least once so a random pouring difference is not mistaken for a grind effect.

### Example log (format only)

| Setting | End of pour | Drawdown done | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One step finer | 1:50 | 3:20 | Sweet, slightly drying finish |
| Baseline | 1:50 | 2:55 | Balanced, clear acidity |
| One step coarser | 1:50 | 2:30 | Clean but thin |

A log like this helps you decide far more than "this grinder is good".

## Deciding what to do next

- **Slow and drying**: swirl less first, or grind slightly coarser.
- **Fast and thin**: grind slightly finer.
- **Normal flow but sour and drying together**: check pouring evenness, or consider the grinder's distribution.
- **Already balanced**: keep it, even if the time does not match someone else's recipe.

## Limits of grinder conversion charts

Barista Flow and many websites offer charts converting settings between grinders. They give a starting point but cannot replace your own calibration because:

- burrs wear over time;
- each grinder's zero point can differ slightly;
- assembly and cleanliness affect grinding;
- beans differ in density and roast level.

Your most valuable calibration tools are still a scale, timing notes and blind comparisons.

## Maintenance affects distribution too

- **Clean regularly**: old oils and stale grounds affect grinding and add stale flavours. Clean the burrs as the manufacturer instructs.
- **Watch for static**: static makes fines cling to the chute or cup and causes dose errors. Some people add a tiny amount of water to beans before grinding to reduce static; check that your grinder's manufacturer allows it first.
- **Check for burr wear**: after long use, the same setting may grind coarser and flow faster.

## FAQ

**Do I need an expensive grinder?**
Not necessarily. A reliable entry-level burr grinder usually improves the cup more than any blade grinder or pre-ground coffee. Consistency and fine adjustment matter most.

**Does sieving improve flavour?**
Some enthusiasts sieve out fines or boulders. It is a useful research tool, but it wastes coffee and changes extraction. For most people, stabilising pouring and grind settings is more effective.

**Why is flow different this week at the same setting?**
Degassing, room humidity, bean temperature at grinding and natural ageing of the beans can all change flow. That is why your log should include days since opening.
