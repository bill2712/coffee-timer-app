## The value of stages is repeatability

Splitting the total water into stages does not automatically make coffee sweeter. Its real value is creating checkpoints: how much cumulative water you should have at a given time, whether the slurry level is too high, and whether flow has drifted from last time.

That lets you locate where a problem happened rather than only seeing the final time. Two cups might both finish at 3:00, but one was slowed by the bloom and the other by a clogged finish, and the fixes are completely different.

## The overall structure

Barista Flow's default rhythm uses cumulative targets, not per-stage amounts:

| Stage | Target time | Cumulative water | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1: Bloom | 0:00–0:40 | Dose × 2.5 | Wet evenly and release gas |
| 2: Main pour | 0:40–1:30 | About 60% of total | Build the slurry and do most of the extraction |
| 3: Finish | 1:30–2:30 | 100% of total | Reach the target strength |

Then wait for the water to drain completely. Total time varies with grind, brewer and coffee; 2:30–3:30 is common.

### Pour table at 1:16

| 12 g | 192 g | 30 g | 115 g | 192 g |

| 20 g | 320 g | 50 g | 192 g | 320 g |
| 25 g | 400 g | 63 g | 240 g | 400 g |

Values are rounded. The scale shows cumulative weight, so at the end of stage two with a 15 g dose it should read 144 g, not "add another 144 g".

## Stage one: even wetting and degassing

The bloom wets the grounds with a small amount of water. Barista Flow defaults to 2.5 times the dose and a 40-second wait; for example, pour to 50 g for 20 g of coffee.

**Key points:**

- Start in the centre and spiral outwards so all grounds turn dark.
- If dry patches remain, add a little water or give one gentle swirl.
- Older coffee releases less gas and may not need the full 40 seconds.
- Very fresh coffee rises a lot; a slightly longer bloom can help.

**Signs of trouble:** dry clumps floating on top, bloom water running straight down the edges, or a sunken centre with dry edges.

## Stage two: building the main slurry

The next stage brings cumulative water to about 60% of the total. This is the largest pour and where most extraction happens.

**Key points:**

- Keep a steady stream in circles, avoiding pouring directly onto the paper.
- A higher, faster or wider stream usually means more agitation.
- Keep the slurry at a steady level without exposing the bed or nearly overflowing.

**Signs of trouble:** the level rises too high and drains very slowly. Do not immediately stir hard; that can push more fines into the filter. Keep the stream steady and test a coarser grind or less swirling next time.

## Stage three: reaching the target strength

The final stage pours to the total weight. The goal is to complete the recipe, not to "wash out every last bit of flavour".

**Key points:**

- After pouring, a single gentle swirl can flatten the bed; no need to be forceful.
- Note when drawdown finishes.

**Signs of trouble:** the finish often tastes drying, or drawdown is clearly longer than last time. Compare versions with less total water, a coarser grind, a lower temperature or removing the dripper earlier. Change one thing at a time to know which helped.

## Are more pours better?

Some recipes use four, five or more pours. More pours mean smaller amounts each time, with different agitation and slurry levels. More pours are not automatically better; it is simply another way to control the brew.

For beginners, three stages strike a good balance: enough checkpoints without so many steps that you lose track. Once comfortable, try splitting stage two into two pours and compare.

## Reading Barista Flow's curve

The timer shows cumulative targets: bloom water by 40 seconds, 60% of total by 90 seconds and the final weight by 150 seconds. The curve does not read your scale and does not mean you must pour in a continuous straight line.

Use it like a metronome and check your scale at each checkpoint. If you are at 130 g at 1:30 with a 144 g target, start stage two slightly earlier next time or pour a little faster.

## One effective test

1. Brew the same recipe twice in a row to confirm your technique is repeatable.
2. If the two total times differ by more than 20–30 seconds, work on pouring consistency before blaming beans or grinder.
3. Once stable, change one variable, for example moving the end of stage two from 60% to 70%, and compare the taste.

## FAQ

**Can I do staged pouring without a gooseneck kettle?**
Yes, but control is harder. A regular kettle produces a thick, fast stream with more agitation. Pour lower and slower, and keep it consistent.

**Do I need a scale?**
Staged pouring is built on cumulative water. Without a scale it is very hard to know whether you reached each target. A scale is the first tool worth buying.

**Does this rhythm work for iced pour-over?**
Iced pour-over replaces part of the water with ice in the server, so there is less hot water and a stronger concentrate. The staging idea still works, but total hot water and grind need separate adjustment.
