## Why no single temperature suits every coffee

You may have read that "the best pour-over temperature is 92°C". That number can be a starting point, but it will not suit every coffee.

Water temperature affects how quickly soluble compounds enter the water, but it is not an independent variable. Grind, contact time, ratio, how well the brewer holds heat and water chemistry all shape the result together. The same 92°C water poured into a preheated and an unheated brewer produces different bed temperatures.

Roast level is one of the main factors that decides how much extraction energy a coffee needs.

## How roasting changes the bean

During roasting, green coffee loses moisture, expands and becomes more porous, while many chemical reactions such as the Maillard reaction and caramelisation create aroma and colour. The darker the roast, the further these changes go:

| Roast | Structure | Solubility | Typical flavour direction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Light | Denser, harder | Slower | Bright acidity, floral, fruity |
| Medium | In between | Moderate | Sweetness, nuts, caramel; softer acidity |
| Dark | More porous, brittle | Faster | Bittersweet, cocoa, smoky, heavy body |

So light roasts usually need more extraction energy (hotter water, finer grind or longer time), while dark roasts are easier to over-extract.

## Roast level gives you a direction

| Roast | Common starting temperature | If sharp and thin | If bitter and drying |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Light | 93–96°C | Grind finer first, then raise temperature | Agitate less or grind coarser |
| Medium | 90–93°C | One step finer | One step coarser or 2°C cooler |
| Dark | 85–90°C | Slightly hotter or finer | Cooler, coarser or shorter |

These are starting points only. Variety, roast development, freshness and the flavour you want can all move the best setting. Some light roasts taste cleaner a little cooler; some dark roasts become sweeter a little hotter.

The brewer matters too. The official AeroPress everyday method uses about 85°C, which differs from typical pour-over starting points. It shows that brew method and roast level must be considered together. Immersion brewers keep grounds in contact with water for longer, so they can reach adequate extraction at lower temperatures.

## How to judge roast level

"Light", "medium" and "dark" on a bag are not standardised; every roaster defines them differently. Combine several clues:

- **Bag description**: tasting notes hint at direction. "Jasmine, lemon" usually suggests lighter; "dark chocolate, smoky" usually suggests darker.
- **Appearance**: darker beans may look oily; lighter beans look dry and matte.
- **Grinding feel**: light roasts are usually harder to grind.
- **Bloom**: fresh darker roasts often release more gas.

These are clues; brewing and tasting are the most reliable test.

## A suggested adjustment process

1. Fix dose, ratio, grind and pouring method.
2. Choose a reasonable start from the table above.
3. Change temperature by 2–3°C between brews.
4. Compare cups at a similar drinking temperature, because heat hides some sweetness and acidity.
5. If the cup is sharp and thin with normal flow, try hotter water. If it is drying and bitter, try cooler water.
6. Once you find a balanced range, fine-tune grind rather than changing everything at once.

### Why 2–3°C?

Changes that are too small (say 0.5°C) are often smaller than the error from your brewer and pouring, so you will not taste them. Changes that are too large (say 10°C) can skip right past the best range. Two to three degrees usually produces a noticeable difference without overshooting.

## Easy-to-miss sources of error

- **Preheating**: an unheated dripper and server lower the real temperature noticeably.
- **Kettle versus bed temperature**: the kettle display shows water inside the kettle.
- **Altitude**: water boils at a lower temperature at altitude.
- **Pour timing**: different gaps between pours change the average temperature over the brew.
- **Dose**: small doses lose heat faster.

Barista Flow's temperatures are starting points and do not measure your real bed temperature. Put the cup and your notes ahead of the numbers.

## A practical example

Say you buy a washed light-roast Ethiopian coffee described as "jasmine, lemon, black tea":

1. Cup one: 94°C, 1:16, medium-fine. Sharp acidity, thin body.
2. Cup two: grind one small step finer only. Acidity softens and sweetness appears.
3. Cup three: same grind, raise to 96°C. Aroma fuller, but slightly drying at the end.
4. Conclusion: keep cup two's grind and 94–95°C as the baseline for this coffee.

Each cup changed only one variable, so every conclusion can be traced.

## FAQ

**Must dark roasts be brewed cool?**
Not necessarily. Cooler water is a common way to reduce bitterness, but if it makes the cup thin, try a higher temperature with a coarser grind.

**Should I use a different temperature in summer and winter?**
Room temperature affects how fast your brewer cools, and winter cools things faster. If winter cups taste thinner, improve preheating before changing the recipe.

**What temperature for a "medium-dark" roast?**
Start around 89–91°C and adjust by taste. Roast names are not standardised, so rely on the description and what you taste.
