## Why does the same recipe taste different with different water?

A cup of filter coffee is more than 98% water. If the same beans, grinder and recipe taste sweet at home but flat or sharp at a friend's place, water is one of the most commonly overlooked causes.

Many people judge water by the number on a TDS pen. TDS tells you how much is dissolved in the water, not what it is. For brewing, three groups of components matter most:

- **Calcium and magnesium** take part in pulling coffee compounds into the water and are the main cause of scale.
- **Bicarbonate (alkalinity)** decides how much "buffering" the water has to neutralise coffee's acids.
- **Other ions and impurities**, such as sodium, chlorine and disinfection by-products, can add salty or off flavours.

Two waters with a similar TDS can have very different mineral profiles, and so can brew very different cups.

## Three ideas to keep separate

### Total hardness

Hardness mostly comes from calcium and magnesium and is often reported as "mg/L as CaCO₃". These minerals influence how efficiently and evenly coffee extracts, but harder is not automatically better. Very hard water scales kettles and machines quickly and can make a cup taste dull or chalky.

### Alkalinity

Alkalinity describes how strongly water resists a change in acidity. In brewing water it comes mainly from bicarbonate. Coffee is acidic, so the higher the alkalinity, the more bright acidity gets "buffered" away:

- **High alkalinity**: acidity turns round and flat; the fruit in light roasts is easily muted.
- **Low alkalinity**: there is little buffering, so acidity can taste sharp and thin.

Alkalinity is related to pH but is not the same thing. A single pH reading cannot tell you how much buffering capacity water has.

### TDS / conductivity

Most TDS pens measure electrical conductivity and convert it to ppm with a fixed factor. They are useful for tracking whether the same water source has changed, but they cannot distinguish calcium from magnesium or bicarbonate, so they are not enough on their own to predict flavour.

## How to use the SCA water standard

The Specialty Coffee Association's water standard gives target values and acceptable ranges. Commonly cited targets include:

| Parameter | Approximate SCA target | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total dissolved solids | About 150 mg/L | Too low or too high can push extraction and body off balance |
| Calcium hardness | About 68 mg/L as CaCO₃ | Affects extraction and scale risk |
| Total alkalinity | About 40 mg/L as CaCO₃ | Affects how much acidity is neutralised |
| pH | About 7.0 | Large deviations often signal other issues |
| Chlorine | 0 mg/L | Chlorine flavours go straight into the cup |

These are industry reference points, not a guarantee that a cup will taste good. Their main use is to show which direction your water leans, so you can check the result in the cup.

## Where to start at home

### Check your water supplier's data

Most water utilities publish regular water quality reports that include hardness, alkalinity and pH for each supply zone. In Hong Kong, for example, the Water Supplies Department publishes this data, and the supply is generally on the soft side. Building tanks, pipes and filters can still change what comes out of your tap.

### Read bottled water labels

Bottled water labels usually list calcium, magnesium, bicarbonate and total dissolved solids. When reading them:

1. **Check the units.** Some use mg/L and others ppm; for dilute water they are roughly equivalent.
2. **Be careful with high-bicarbonate mineral water.** High alkalinity often makes light roasts taste flat.
3. **Avoid relying on distilled or near-mineral-free water alone.** It can taste hollow or sharp, and water labelled only for industrial or appliance use should not be drunk.

### What filters do

Activated carbon filters mainly reduce chlorine and some off-flavours and have limited effect on hardness. Ion-exchange cartridges can change both hardness and alkalinity. If the taste changes noticeably after you replace a cartridge, the beans may not be the cause, so note the change in your log.

## A fair home comparison

The most convincing test changes only the water and keeps everything else fixed:

1. Pick two or three sources, such as filtered tap water, a low-mineral bottled water and a moderate-mineral bottled water.
2. Use the same coffee, dose, grind, temperature and pouring pattern.
3. Rinse the kettle and brewer thoroughly between sources.
4. Record total brew time. If flow differs a lot, check that your pouring was consistent.
5. Let the cups cool to a similar temperature and, ideally, have someone shuffle them for a blind tasting.
6. Note acidity clarity, sweetness, dryness and finish rather than just "good" or "bad".
7. Repeat on another day. Do not draw conclusions from a difference you cannot reproduce.

### Example log format

| Water | Total time | Acidity | Sweetness | Body | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Filtered tap | 3:05 | Clear | Medium | Slightly light | Baseline |
| Bottled A (low mineral) | 3:00 | Sharp | Low | Thin | Short acidity |
| Bottled B (high bicarbonate) | 3:10 | Muted | Medium | Fuller | Fruit nearly gone |

The values only illustrate the format; they are not test results. Your own results are what you should adjust from.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Lower TDS is purer, so it is better."** Very low-mineral water does not necessarily brew a balanced cup and is not inherently healthier.
- **"Mineral water has more minerals, so it tastes better."** High bicarbonate often neutralises acidity, especially in light roasts.
- **"New water means a completely new recipe."** Compare with your existing recipe first, then fine-tune grind or temperature once you know the direction.

## Safety and equipment

- Do not add laboratory or industrial salts to water without understanding purity, dose and intended use. If you want to try a commercial mineral packet made for coffee, follow the product instructions.
- Do not drink water labelled only for distillation, batteries or industrial use.
- Descale kettles and machines according to the manufacturer's instructions. For boiler-based machines, the water needed to protect the equipment may differ from your taste preference for filter coffee; follow the manufacturer first.

## FAQ

**Do I need a TDS pen?**
Not necessarily. If you always use the same source, a pen mainly tracks change. Utility reports and bottle labels are a good starting point.

**Why does café coffee taste better than mine?**
Many reasons, including freshness, grinders and technique, and water is one of them. Many cafés use dedicated filtration to control hardness and alkalinity.

**Can Barista Flow analyse my water?**
No. Barista Flow does not measure water quality; it only calculates from the values you enter. Add the water source to your brew log so you can compare later.
