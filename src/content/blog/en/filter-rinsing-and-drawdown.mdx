## How one sheet of paper affects the whole cup

In pour-over, the filter is the only way out for water leaving the coffee. It decides how fast water can drain, how many fines are held back and whether the cup tastes of paper. Many "why did this take four minutes?" problems end up being about the filter or the coffee bed, not the beans.

This guide covers what rinsing really does and then gives a troubleshooting sequence for slow drawdowns.

## Four reasons to rinse the filter

Rinsing with hot water is the first step in most pour-over methods, and HARIO's published V60 technique includes it as part of preparation. It helps to:

1. **Reduce paper taste**: wash away loose fibres and noticeable paper flavour. Unbleached filters usually taste more papery.
2. **Preheat the brewer**: warm the dripper and server so less heat is lost during brewing.
3. **Seat the filter**: a wet filter clings to the dripper walls so water follows the path the brewer was designed for.
4. **Check the fold**: you can see tears or an uneven fold before adding coffee.

Remember to discard the rinse water, or it will dilute your coffee.

## Bleached versus unbleached filters

| Type | Characteristics | Advice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bleached (white) | Usually less paper taste | Still rinse to preheat and seat |
| Unbleached (brown) | Paper taste can be more noticeable | Rinse with more hot water |

Thickness, texture and porosity vary widely between brands, so even two "V60 02" filters can flow differently. Treat a new filter as a new variable.

## Rinsing does not fix every flow problem

Filter material, thickness, fold and seating all affect drainage. When comparing filters, keep grind and pouring fixed rather than changing the recipe the moment total time differs.

Another common issue: after rinsing, the filter is pulled tight against the dripper and blocks the outlet. This is more common with some flat-bottom drippers or poor folds.

## Record three flow checkpoints

Instead of only noting "finished at 3:30", record:

1. **End of bloom** (e.g. 0:40)
2. **End of the last pour** (e.g. 1:50)
3. **End of drawdown** (e.g. 3:10)

The time between points 2 and 3 is the drawdown. It best reflects the state of the bed and filter:

| Pattern | Likely cause |
| --- | --- |
| Normal at first, then suddenly very slow | Fines migration or a clogged filter |
| Slow from the very first pour | Grind too fine, filter blocking the outlet, or dose too large for the brewer |
| Very different every time | Inconsistent pouring or grinding |
| Fast overall and thin-tasting | Grind too coarse or pouring concentrated in the centre |

## Troubleshooting a slow finish

1. **Reduce agitation first**: on the next cup, swirl or stir one time fewer and keep everything else the same.
2. **Then grind coarser**: if it is still clearly slow, go one small step coarser.
3. **Check where you pour**: are you repeatedly hitting the same spot hard, pushing fines together?
4. **Compare filters side by side**: record flow and taste, not just seconds.
5. **Make sure the filter is not sealing the outlet**: check the fold and look for suction between paper and dripper.

Coffee ad Astra explains that excessive agitation can push fines into the paper's pores until only a few channels remain. A longer time is just the symptom; the real issue is uneven flow. On the other hand, a longer brew without clogging is not necessarily bad, and taste remains the final judge.

## A practical comparison

Brew two cups of the same recipe back to back:

- **Cup one**: one gentle swirl after each pour, or none at all.
- **Cup two**: swirl or stir after every pour.

Record the time from the last pour to the end of drawdown and how drying each cup tastes. If cup two finishes much later and tastes drier, reduce agitation first instead of changing filter, grind and temperature all at once.

## What the spent coffee bed tells you

After brewing, look at the grounds in the filter:

- **Flat and even**: usually means even flow.
- **Deep crater in the middle, high walls**: pouring may be too concentrated in the centre, or not enough swirl.
- **Higher on one side**: the dripper may not be level, or you poured off-centre.
- **Lots of dry grounds stuck high on the walls**: some coffee did not take part in extraction.

Bed shape is not definitive, but combined with flow and taste it often points to the problem.

## FAQ

**Do I have to rinse the filter?**
In most cases it is recommended. Even if paper taste does not bother you, preheating and seating help flow consistency.

**What about cloth or metal filters?**
They behave differently. Metal filters have larger openings, so more oils and fines reach the cup, giving more body but less clarity, and they usually flow faster. Re-dial your grind when switching.

**Why does flow vary within the same box of filters?**
Folding, placement in the dripper and rinsing all affect how the filter seats. Folding and rinsing the same way every time reduces the variation.
