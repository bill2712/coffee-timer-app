## Start by describing what you taste

"This cup is bad" does not help the next cup. To fix a problem, break the impression down. When tasting, try to answer separately:

1. **Acidity**: bright and clear, or sharp and harsh?
2. **Sweetness**: is there any? At the start, middle or finish?
3. **Bitterness**: rounded like dark chocolate, or harsh like charcoal?
4. **Body**: watery or full and weighty?
5. **Finish**: how long does it last after swallowing? Pleasantly sweet, or drying and scratchy?

Taste once the coffee is warm rather than hot. Hot coffee dulls your perception of acidity and sweetness, and many problems only show as the cup cools.

## Good acidity versus sour under-extraction

Many beginners assume any acidity means a mistake. Acidity is an important feature of specialty coffee, especially light roasts. The key is telling them apart:

| | Good acidity | Under-extracted sourness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feels | Bright, juicy | Sharp, harsh, unripe |
| Accompanied by | Supporting sweetness | No sweetness; hollow body |
| Finish | Clean and pleasant | Short, sometimes salty |
| Like | Citrus, berries | Vinegar, unripe fruit |

If the acidity makes you want another sip, it is probably good. If it makes you wince, you may need more extraction.

## Roast bitterness versus over-extraction

Bitterness is not always wrong either. Dark roasts naturally carry more bitterness as part of their style.

| | Roast bitterness | Over-extracted bitterness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feels | Rounded, like cocoa or caramel | Harsh, rough |
| Accompanied by | Often sweetness | Often dryness and astringency |
| Finish | Bittersweet balance | Drying, scratchy, lingering |

Dryness is the most useful clue for over-extraction. If your mouth feels sucked dry and the sides of your tongue tighten after swallowing, consider reducing extraction.

## Eight common problems and what to change

| You taste | Likely cause | Change only this next time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sour, thin, short | Under-extraction | Grind one small step finer |
| Bitter, drying, slow drawdown | Over-extraction or fines clogging | Grind one small step coarser |
| Balanced but watery | Strength too low | Stronger ratio (e.g. 1:16 → 1:15) |
| Heavy and intense, not bitter or drying | Strength too high | Weaker ratio (e.g. 1:15 → 1:16) |
| Sour and drying in the same cup | Uneven flow, channelling | Agitate less; check pouring is not concentrated in one spot |
| Flat, little aroma | Stale beans or poor storage | Check roast date and storage |
| Papery | Filter not rinsed | Rinse the filter thoroughly with hot water |
| Salty or odd flavours | Water, or severe under-extraction | Compare a different water, then adjust grind |

### Why grind first?

Grind has the most direct effect on extraction and is the easiest to undo. Change it by one small step and the next cup shows whether the direction is right. Temperature and pouring are harder to isolate, so leave them until the grind direction is clear.

## A worked example

**Situation**: a medium-roast Colombian coffee, 15 g, 240 g water, 92°C, finished at 2:30. The cup is sharp and thin.

1. **Diagnosis**: sharp and thin with fast flow, suggesting under-extraction.
2. **Cup two**: grind one small step finer only. Finishes at 2:50; acidity softens and caramel sweetness appears.
3. **Cup three**: repeat the setting. Similar result, confirming the change worked.
4. **Cup four**: balanced but a little weak, so change the ratio to 1:15.
5. **Conclusion**: keep the finer grind and 1:15 as this coffee's baseline.

Four cups, and every step has a reason.

## "Sour and bitter at once" is easy to misread

When a cup is sharp and drying at the same time, many people swing the grind back and forth and make things worse. This is usually not overall over- or under-extraction but **uneven extraction**: some grounds barely touched by water, others over-extracted.

Common causes:

- dry clumps left after the bloom;
- a stream too concentrated in the centre or too close to the paper;
- too much swirling or stirring, clogging the filter with fines;
- a dripper that is not level;
- a blade grinder producing a very wide range of particle sizes.

Fix these technique issues first, then return to grind.

## Do not overlook the beans

Some "problems" come from the coffee rather than brewing:

- **Old or poorly stored beans** taste flat no matter how you adjust.
- **Very freshly roasted beans** degas heavily, so flow and taste change daily.
- **Style**: dark roasts are bitter and light roasts are acidic. If that is not your preference, changing coffee may help more than changing the recipe.

## FAQ

**Can sugar or milk fix sourness or bitterness?**
They can mask it but will not fix extraction. If you like milk, brew a balanced cup first and then add to taste.

**How can I train my palate?**
Side-by-side comparison works best: the same coffee ground slightly finer and slightly coarser, tasted together. Differences are much clearer next to each other.

**Do I need to take notes every time?**
Take notes for the first few cups of a new bag. Once you have a baseline, only note changes when the taste clearly shifts.
