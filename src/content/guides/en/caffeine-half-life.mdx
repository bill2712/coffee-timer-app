If you have ever wondered why one friend can drink espresso after dinner and sleep soundly while another is wide awake after a 3 p.m. latte, the answer usually starts with one number: **caffeine half-life**. This guide explains what that number means, what a typical value is, and the main things that change it — so you can choose a sensible setting in the [caffeine calculator](/en/caffeine-calculator/) and understand its results.

## What “half-life” means

The half-life of caffeine is the time it takes for your body to remove **half of the caffeine that is currently in it**. It describes a rate, not a fixed amount. After one half-life, half is left; after two, a quarter; after three, an eighth; and so on.

Here is what that looks like for a 100 mg cup of coffee with a 5-hour half-life, using the same model as our calculator (which also includes about 45 minutes for absorption, so the starting point is slightly below 100 mg):

| Time after drinking | Estimated caffeine left |
| --- | --- |
| 45 minutes (peak) | about 90 mg |
| 5 hours | about 52 mg |
| 10 hours | about 26 mg |
| 15 hours | about 13 mg |
| 20 hours | about 6 mg |
| 25 hours | about 3 mg |

Two practical points follow from this:

- **Caffeine never disappears all at once.** It fades gradually, so a noon coffee can still be present — in a smaller amount — at midnight.
- **Doses stack.** If you drink a second cup before the first one has cleared, the second one starts on top of what is left. Three cups spread across a day can leave more in your body at bedtime than one large cup in the morning.

A common rule of thumb is that after about five half-lives a substance is largely gone (around 3% remains). With a 5-hour half-life, that is roughly a full day.

## How long is a typical caffeine half-life?

For healthy adults, a half-life of **around 5 hours** is often used as a typical average. The Institute of Medicine’s review of caffeine pharmacology describes a mean plasma half-life of about 5 hours, with a wide range of roughly **1.5 to 9.5 hours** between individuals. Caffeine is absorbed almost completely, with peak blood levels usually between 15 and 120 minutes after drinking.

That range is the important part. Someone with a 3-hour half-life clears a morning coffee by mid-afternoon; someone with a 7-hour half-life still has a meaningful amount at night. That is why the calculator offers three plain-language presets — fast (about 3 hours), average (about 5 hours) and slow (about 7 hours) — plus a slider from 2 to 10 hours.

## What changes your caffeine half-life

About 95% of caffeine is broken down in the liver by an enzyme called **CYP1A2**. Anything that speeds up or slows down this enzyme, or the liver itself, changes how long caffeine lasts. Pharmacology reviews (for example Nehlig, 2018) list the following factors.

### Genetics

Differences in the *CYP1A2* gene and the genes that regulate it help explain why some people are naturally “fast” or “slow” caffeine metabolisers. Genetics also affects how sensitive your brain is to caffeine — through adenosine receptors — which is a different thing from how fast you clear it. Genetic test results can be interesting, but they do not replace how you actually feel and sleep, and they are not a reason to ignore medical advice.

### Smoking

Tobacco smoke strongly *increases* CYP1A2 activity, so smokers usually clear caffeine considerably faster than non-smokers. The flip side matters: when people **stop smoking**, the same number of coffees can lead to noticeably higher caffeine levels, which may add to jitteriness or poor sleep while quitting. If you have recently quit, consider moving the half-life setting towards “average” or “slow”.

### Pregnancy

Pregnancy slows caffeine clearance, and the effect grows as pregnancy progresses. Estimates for the third trimester commonly run to around **10–16 hours or more** — two to three times longer than usual. Caffeine also crosses the placenta. For this reason EFSA advises that habitual intake of up to **200 mg per day** does not raise safety concerns for the fetus, rather than the 400 mg used for other adults, and the Hong Kong Centre for Food Safety advises pregnant and breastfeeding women to watch their intake. If you are pregnant, follow your doctor’s or midwife’s advice rather than any calculator.

### Hormonal contraception

Oral contraceptives that contain estrogen inhibit CYP1A2. Studies have found that they can **roughly double** caffeine half-life (an estimate; the size of the effect varies). If you use hormonal contraception and notice that coffee lasts longer for you than for others, this may be part of the reason.

### Certain medications

Some medicines block CYP1A2 and can make caffeine last **several times longer**. Well-known examples include the antidepressant **fluvoxamine** and some antibiotics such as **ciprofloxacin**. Other drugs interact with caffeine in the opposite direction or have their own effects on heart rate and sleep. Do not stop or change any medication because of caffeine — instead, ask your pharmacist or doctor whether you should limit caffeine while taking it.

### Liver function

Because the liver does almost all of the work, liver disease can prolong caffeine half-life dramatically — in severe cases to well over a day (an estimate). People with liver conditions should get personalised advice.

### Age and other factors

Newborn babies have immature liver enzymes and clear caffeine extremely slowly, with half-lives measured in days. In healthy adults, age, body size, diet, alcohol and exercise have smaller or less consistent effects. These factors are part of why two people drinking the same cup can end up with different levels hours later.

## How to choose a half-life for yourself

You cannot measure your own half-life at home, but you can make a reasonable choice:

1. **Start with 5 hours** unless one of the factors above clearly applies.
2. **Notice your patterns.** If coffee after lunch regularly makes it hard to fall asleep, try 7 hours. If you smoke, or caffeine seems to wear off quickly for you, try 3 hours.
3. **Use the chart to test your choice.** Log a typical day and compare the result with how you actually slept. If the calculator says “good” but you lie awake, move the half-life up or lower your sleep threshold.
4. **Do not use the slider to self-diagnose.** If you are pregnant, take medication or have a health condition, your real half-life may be outside the 2–10 hour range — ask a professional.

## Half-life is not the same as sensitivity

A long half-life means caffeine stays in your body longer. **Sensitivity** means how strongly you respond to a given amount. Some people clear caffeine quickly but still feel anxious after one strong espresso; others clear it slowly but report few effects. Both matter for sleep. The calculator handles half-life with the slider and sensitivity with the **sleep threshold** setting: lower the threshold if small amounts affect you.

## Talk to your doctor

This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor, pharmacist or midwife before relying on caffeine estimates if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, take regular medication (especially antidepressants, antibiotics or heart medicines), have liver, heart or anxiety conditions, or are advising a child or teenager. They can tell you whether a lower daily limit or a different cutoff time makes sense for you.
