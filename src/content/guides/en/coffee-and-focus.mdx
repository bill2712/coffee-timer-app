For many people, the first step of any focus session is making a coffee. That instinct has a real basis: caffeine is one of the most studied substances for alertness and attention. But more coffee is not more focus, and the timing of your cup matters at least as much as its size. This guide explains what caffeine can and cannot do for concentration, how to time it around a work session, and how to avoid paying for today’s focus with tonight’s sleep. To see your own numbers, use the [caffeine calculator](/en/caffeine-calculator/).

## What caffeine does in the brain

Throughout the day a substance called **adenosine** builds up in the brain. It is one of the signals behind “sleep pressure”: the longer you have been awake, the more adenosine there is and the more tired you feel. Caffeine works mainly by **blocking adenosine receptors**. The adenosine is still there, but its message is muffled, so you feel more awake and find it easier to keep your attention on a task.

The effect starts within minutes of drinking and is usually strongest somewhere between 15 minutes and 2 hours later, as caffeine is absorbed. It then fades slowly: with a typical half-life of around five hours, about half of a morning coffee is still in your body at lunch. Our [caffeine half-life guide](/en/guides/caffeine-half-life/) explains why this varies so much between people.

## How much caffeine helps attention

The European Food Safety Authority has assessed this question for health-claim purposes. It concluded that a cause-and-effect relationship has been established between caffeine and **increased alertness and increased attention** in adults, at a dose of **at least 75 mg** per serving. That is roughly a single espresso or a small cup of filter coffee.

A broad 2016 review of caffeine’s effects on cognitive, physical and occupational performance (McLellan, Caldwell and Lieberman) reached a similar picture: moderate doses reliably improve vigilance, reaction time and attention, especially when people are tired or sleep-deprived, which is why caffeine is widely used by shift workers and the military. Higher doses are needed for physical performance than for alertness.

Beyond a moderate dose, the extra benefit for focus is small, while side effects — jitteriness, a racing heart, anxiety, an upset stomach — become more likely. For most adults, one or two normal cups spread sensibly through the working day are plenty. Keep the daily total within the 400 mg reference used by the FDA and EFSA for healthy adults, and much lower if you are pregnant, sensitive to caffeine or advised so by your doctor.

## Timing caffeine around a focus session

Because caffeine takes time to be absorbed, **drink it shortly before you start**, not when you are already struggling halfway through. A practical pattern:

1. **Have your coffee 15–30 minutes before a demanding session.** Use that time to prepare: choose the task, gather materials, put your phone away.
2. **Start the session as the effect builds.** A 25- or 50-minute block lines up well with the period when caffeine is close to its peak.
3. **Use water, not more coffee, during breaks.** Topping up every break adds caffeine faster than your body clears it.
4. **Save your next cup for the next big block**, not for the afternoon slump out of habit.

You may have heard that you should wait 90 minutes after waking before your first coffee, because of morning cortisol. This advice is popular online, but there is little good evidence that delaying coffee by a fixed time improves focus or energy for most people. If you prefer coffee first thing and sleep well, there is no strong reason to change.

## The coffee nap

If you hit an afternoon slump, a **coffee nap** can work surprisingly well: drink a coffee, then immediately take a nap of under 15 minutes. You wake up just as the caffeine starts to act, and the short nap clears some of the sleep pressure. In a 1997 driving-simulator study, sleepy drivers given 200 mg of caffeine plus a short nap showed less sleepiness and fewer driving incidents than with caffeine alone or a placebo. Keep the nap short to avoid waking up groggy — and treat real sleepiness at the wheel as a reason to stop driving, not to drink more coffee.

## Tolerance and withdrawal

If you drink coffee every day, your body adapts. Regular consumers develop some **tolerance** to caffeine’s effects, and skipping your usual dose can cause **withdrawal** — headache, tiredness, low mood and difficulty concentrating — typically starting 12–24 hours after the last cup.

This has led to a long-running scientific debate. Some researchers argue that much of the alertness habitual drinkers feel after their morning coffee is simply the **reversal of mild withdrawal**, returning them to normal rather than above it. Others conclude that caffeine still offers a modest net benefit, especially when people are tired. The practical takeaway is the same either way:

- **Keep intake moderate and fairly consistent**, so you are not riding a cycle of highs and withdrawal dips.
- **If you want to cut down, taper gradually** over a week or two to limit withdrawal headaches and foggy days.
- **Do not rely on caffeine to cover chronic sleep loss.** It masks tiredness; it does not replace sleep.

## Protecting tonight’s sleep

The biggest cost of poorly timed coffee is usually not felt today but tonight. Caffeine taken in the afternoon can shorten and lighten sleep, and poor sleep makes tomorrow’s focus harder — which tempts you to drink more coffee. A 2023 meta-analysis suggested having a typical cup of coffee at least 8–9 hours before bed. Our guide to [caffeine and sleep](/en/guides/caffeine-and-sleep/) explains the research and practical cutoff times, and the [caffeine calculator](/en/caffeine-calculator/) estimates the latest time you can have one more cup.

## Coffee is a helper, not a strategy

Caffeine makes it easier to stay alert; it does not decide what you work on or protect you from interruptions. The biggest gains in focus usually come from the basics: enough sleep, one clear task at a time, fewer notifications, and regular breaks. A good routine might look like this: a coffee at 9:00, a 50-minute focus block at 9:15 with the phone in another room, a real break, another block, and water rather than a third coffee in the afternoon.

If you find you need more and more caffeine to function, feel anxious or shaky after small amounts, or struggle with concentration despite good sleep, talk to a doctor.
