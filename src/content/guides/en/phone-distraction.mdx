Most of us do not decide to check our phones; we simply find the phone in our hand. That is not a character flaw. Phones are designed to be interesting, notifications are designed to be noticed, and checking is quick and occasionally rewarding — the perfect recipe for a habit. The good news is that habits respond to environment. This guide explains what research shows about phones and attention, where the evidence is weaker than headlines suggest, and practical changes you can make today. A [Phone Down Challenge](/en/focus/) in Brew Focus is one easy way to practise.

## Why your phone is so hard to ignore

Three features make phones uniquely distracting:

- **Unpredictable rewards.** Sometimes a notification is important or pleasant, often it is not. Rewards that arrive unpredictably are especially effective at building checking habits.
- **Low effort.** Unlocking takes a second. Anything that easy will be done often, especially when the task in front of you is hard or boring.
- **Constant cues.** Buzzes, banners, badges and even the sight of the phone remind you that something might be waiting.

None of this means phones are bad. It means that relying on willpower alone is a losing strategy. Changing the environment works better.

## What research shows

**Notifications cost attention even when you do not respond.** In a 2015 experiment published in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance*, 166 students did a demanding attention task. Those who received a call or text notification during the task — without answering it — made significantly more errors than those who received none. The authors linked the effect to mind-wandering: a notification starts thoughts about what it might be.

**Silencing notifications reduces inattention.** In a 2016 field experiment by Kushlev, Proulx and Dunn, 221 students spent one week with phone alerts on and the phone within reach, and another week with alerts off and the phone out of reach. During the alerts-on week they reported significantly more symptoms of inattention and hyperactivity. The authors were careful to say this does not mean phones cause ADHD — it shows that interruptions affect everyday attention.

**Batching notifications can help.** A 2019 randomised field experiment by Fitz and colleagues delivered notifications either as usual, in three batches per day, hourly, or not at all. People who received **three batches a day** reported better mood, more attention and productivity, more control over their phones, and unlocked them less often; hourly batching brought few benefits. Interestingly, turning notifications off entirely made people more anxious about missing out — suggesting that predictable, limited checking works better than total abstinence for many people.

**Interruptions make work more stressful.** A 2008 study by Gloria Mark and colleagues found that people who were interrupted completed tasks faster, with similar quality, but reported more stress, frustration and time pressure. Speed is not the same as wellbeing.

## Where the evidence is weaker than headlines

You may have read that **the mere presence of your phone** — face down, switched off — drains your brainpower. That claim comes from a widely cited 2017 study by Ward and colleagues. However, a pre-registered direct replication published in 2022 did not find the effect, and other replication attempts have also reported null results, while some later reviews still report supporting studies. The honest summary is that the evidence is **mixed**.

That does not make “put the phone in another room” bad advice: it removes the cue and the temptation, and it makes checking require effort. Just be wary of precise claims about how many IQ points a phone costs you.

## Practical tips that work with your habits

### Change what the phone does

- **Turn off non-essential notifications.** Keep calls and messages from people who matter; switch off news, shopping, games and most social apps. You can still open them — they just stop interrupting you.
- **Batch the rest.** Both iOS (Notification Summary) and Android (notification settings and Digital Wellbeing) let you deliver many notifications at scheduled times rather than instantly.
- **Use a focus or Do Not Disturb mode** during work and study hours, with exceptions for genuinely urgent contacts.
- **Remove badges** (the red counters on app icons). They are designed to make you open the app.
- **Move tempting apps** off your home screen, into a folder on the last page. One extra swipe is often enough friction to stop an automatic check.

### Change where the phone is

- **Out of reach, out of sight.** Put the phone in a bag, a drawer or another room during focus time. Whatever the debate about mere presence, distance makes checking a choice rather than a reflex.
- **Charge it outside the bedroom.** Use a cheap alarm clock instead. This also protects your sleep.
- **Create phone-free zones** — the dinner table, the first half hour of the day, meetings.

### Change how you work

- **Use time-boxed focus blocks.** Decide that for the next 25 or 50 minutes the phone stays down, and that you will check it in the break. A clear end makes it easier to resist now. This is exactly what the Pomodoro rhythm and the Phone Down Challenge provide.
- **Write down the urge.** When you feel the pull to check something, jot it on paper. Check it in the break. Most urges disappear on their own.
- **Plan your checks.** Scheduled check-ins (for example on the hour, or three times a day for social media) satisfy curiosity without constant interruption.
- **Track it gently.** Screen-time reports and Brew Focus’s interruption count in gentle mode show patterns. Use them to adjust, not to judge yourself.

## Making it stick

Change one thing at a time, and make the first step small. A week of turning off non-essential notifications is more valuable than a dramatic “digital detox” that lasts a day. Expect some discomfort — the urge to check is strongest just after you remove a cue — and notice how it fades.

If your phone use feels out of control, causes conflict, or you use it to cope with anxiety or low mood, consider talking to a doctor or counsellor. Tools and tips are helpful, but they are not a replacement for support.
