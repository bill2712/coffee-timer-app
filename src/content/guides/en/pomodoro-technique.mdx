The Pomodoro Technique is probably the best-known focus method in the world, and also one of the most misunderstood. It is not “work for 25 minutes, then check your phone”. Used properly, it is a small system for protecting attention, noticing interruptions and taking breaks before you need them. This guide covers where it came from, how to use it, the variations worth trying and the people it suits best — and you can try any rhythm in [Brew Focus](/en/focus/).

## Where the Pomodoro Technique came from

The technique was developed by **Francesco Cirillo** in the late 1980s, when he was a university student in Italy struggling to concentrate on his studies. He challenged himself to study for just a few minutes without distraction, timing it with a kitchen timer shaped like a tomato — *pomodoro* in Italian. That timer gave the method its name, and each focused interval is still called “a pomodoro”.

Cirillo later refined the method and described it in detail, first in a self-published paper and later in his book *The Pomodoro Technique* (2018). The core has stayed remarkably simple:

1. **Choose a task** you want to work on.
2. **Set a timer for 25 minutes.**
3. **Work on that task only** until the timer rings.
4. **Take a short break** of about 3–5 minutes.
5. **After four pomodoros, take a longer break** of about 15–30 minutes.

What makes it more than a timer is the attitude behind it. A pomodoro is indivisible: if you are interrupted and abandon the task, that pomodoro does not count, and you start a new one. Cirillo also encourages recording what interrupted you, so that over time you learn which distractions come from inside (a sudden urge to check something) and which come from outside (a colleague, a call).

## How to run a session, step by step

**Before you start.** Pick one task and make it concrete. “Write the introduction” works; “do marketing” does not. If a task is bigger than four or five pomodoros, split it. Gather what you need and silence what you do not.

**During the pomodoro.** Work only on the chosen task. When an unrelated thought pops up — an email to send, something to look up — write it down on a scrap of paper and return to the task. You will deal with it in a break or a later pomodoro. This “note it and come back” habit is one of the most useful parts of the method.

**When you are interrupted.** If the interruption is genuinely urgent, stop the pomodoro and deal with it; that pomodoro does not count. If it can wait, use Cirillo’s approach: inform (“I’m in the middle of something”), negotiate a time, and call back later.

**During the break.** Step away. Stretch, refill your water, look into the distance. The break is meant to restore attention, so it works best when it does not involve another screen full of information.

**After each set of four.** Take a longer break. This is also a good moment to look at your list: what got done, what needs re-planning, and whether your estimate of “how many pomodoros” was right.

## What research says about scheduled breaks

There is surprisingly little research on the Pomodoro Technique as a whole, but there is useful evidence on its two ingredients: focused intervals and scheduled breaks.

A 2023 study in the *British Journal of Educational Psychology* compared university students who took **systematic “Pomodoro-style” breaks** with students who decided for themselves when to break. The students on a fixed schedule reported better mood — less fatigue and more motivation — and reached similar task completion in less time. Students who chose their own breaks studied in longer stretches but also took longer breaks. Mental effort and task completion did not differ between the groups, so the main benefit was how people felt and how efficiently they worked, not how much they achieved overall.

A 2022 meta-analysis in *PLOS ONE* looked at **micro-breaks** (breaks of 10 minutes or less) across 22 study samples. Micro-breaks gave small but significant boosts to vigour and reduced fatigue. The effect on performance overall was not significant, appearing mainly for less demanding tasks, and longer breaks tended to help performance more. In other words: short breaks are good for how you feel, and very demanding work may need longer breaks than five minutes.

Taken together, the evidence supports the general idea — planned breaks keep you fresher — without proving that 25 minutes is a magic number.

## Popular variations

The classic 25/5 rhythm is a starting point, not a rule. Common variations include:

- **50/10** — longer focus blocks for writing, programming or study that needs time to “load” into your head. Many people find 25 minutes ends just as they reach full concentration.
- **15/3 or 20/5** — shorter blocks for low-energy days, highly aversive tasks or getting started after a long break. Finishing something small builds momentum.
- **90-minute blocks** — a single long session for deep work, followed by a real break of 15–30 minutes.
- **Flexible pomodoros** — some people let the timer ring but finish the sentence or thought before breaking. That is fine, as long as the break still happens.

## Who the Pomodoro Technique suits

It tends to work well for people who:

- **struggle to start** — 25 minutes feels manageable even for a task you are dreading;
- **work in long, unstructured stretches** — students, writers, freelancers and remote workers often benefit from imposed structure;
- **forget to take breaks** and end the day drained;
- **are often interrupted** and want to see how often, and by what.

It may fit less well if:

- **your work is interrupt-driven by nature** — reception, support or on-call roles cannot ignore incoming requests for 25 minutes;
- **you are already in deep flow** — stopping a productive stretch just because a timer rings can cost more than it saves; use longer blocks;
- **rigid timers make you anxious** — try gentle mode, longer intervals, or simply use the timer as a reminder to break rather than a strict rule.

If you find that you regularly cannot stay focused for even short periods, or that attention problems affect your work, studies or relationships, it is worth talking to a doctor. Timers are helpful habits, not a substitute for care.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping breaks** because you feel productive. The break is part of the method; fatigue arrives later.
- **Checking messages during the break.** It feels like rest but often pulls you into new tasks and makes it harder to restart.
- **Choosing tasks that are too big.** “Finish the thesis” is not a pomodoro task. Break it down.
- **Treating every interruption as failure.** The point is to notice and reduce interruptions, not to never have any.

## Getting started today

Pick one task you have been putting off. Set 25 minutes, put your phone out of reach and start. When the timer rings, take five minutes away from the screen. Do it again.
