“How much caffeine is in a cup of coffee?” sounds like a simple question. The honest answer is: *it depends, often by a factor of two or more*. This guide gives typical values and realistic ranges for common drinks, then explains where the variation comes from so you can make a better estimate for the drinks you actually have. ## Comparison table

All figures are **estimates** for a typical serving. Ranges reflect differences in recipe, brand and serving size; individual drinks can fall outside them.

| Drink | Typical serving | Typical caffeine | Realistic range (estimate) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Espresso, single | ~30 ml | ~63 mg | 50–100 mg | Coffee-shop shots vary widely (see below) |
| Espresso, double | ~60 ml | ~126 mg | 100–200 mg | Many cafés use a double as standard |
| Americano | 2 shots + water | ~126 mg | 63–200 mg | Same caffeine as its shots; water adds none |
| Latte / cappuccino, 1 shot | 1 shot + milk | ~63 mg | 50–100 mg | Milk adds volume, not caffeine |
| Latte / cappuccino, 2 shots | 2 shots + milk | ~126 mg | 100–200 mg | Large sizes often contain 2–3 shots |
| Drip / filter coffee | 240 ml cup | ~95 mg | 70–140 mg | Large café cups can be 350–500 ml |
| Cold brew | 350 ml | ~150–200 mg | 150–200 mg+ | Depends heavily on concentrate strength and dilution |
| Instant coffee | 1–2 tsp in 240 ml | ~60 mg | 30–90 mg | Scales with how many spoonfuls you use |
| Black tea | 240 ml | ~47 mg | 25–70 mg | Longer steeping extracts more |
| Green tea | 240 ml | ~28 mg | 20–45 mg | Matcha is higher because you drink the leaf |
| Cola | 355 ml can | ~34 mg | 30–45 mg | Check the label; “zero” versions vary by brand |
| Energy drink | 250 ml can | ~80 mg | 70–160 mg | Larger cans and “extra” versions can exceed 150 mg |
| Decaf coffee | 240 ml | ~2–5 mg | 2–15 mg | Lower, but not zero |

Values are based on food-composition data such as USDA FoodData Central, health-information summaries such as Mayo Clinic’s caffeine chart, published manufacturer information for canned drinks, and laboratory studies of coffee-shop drinks listed below.

## Why the same coffee can vary so much

Researchers who buy “the same” coffee repeatedly keep finding large differences. When a team in Glasgow analysed 20 espressos from different coffee shops, caffeine per serving ranged from **51 to 322 mg** — more than six-fold. A U.S. study of specialty coffees found brewed coffee servings ranging from about **58 to 259 mg**. Here is where those differences come from.

**Bean species.** Robusta beans typically contain roughly twice as much caffeine as Arabica beans (an estimate; both vary). Many supermarket blends and some espresso blends include Robusta, while most specialty coffee is Arabica.

**How much coffee is used.** This is the biggest lever at home. Caffeine dissolves easily, so doubling the amount of ground coffee in your brewer roughly doubles the caffeine in the cup. A “strong” coffee made with more grounds is not just more flavourful — it has more caffeine.

**Serving size.** A café “large” filter coffee may be twice the volume of a standard 240 ml cup, and a large latte may contain an extra shot. Always think in terms of the whole drink, not “one coffee”.

**Brew method and contact time.** Methods that keep water and coffee in contact longer, such as French press and cold brew, tend to extract caffeine thoroughly. Espresso extracts quickly under pressure but uses a lot of coffee for a small volume.

**Roast level.** Roasting changes caffeine less than many people think. By weight, light and dark roasts are similar; if you measure by scoop, darker beans are less dense, so a scoop can contain slightly less coffee. The difference is usually small compared with dose and cup size.

**Grind size and water temperature.** Finer grinds and hotter water extract faster, but in normal brewing most of the caffeine ends up in the cup either way, so these matter less than dose.

## Espresso versus filter coffee

Espresso is far more concentrated per millilitre, but a single shot is small. A single espresso (around 63 mg) usually has *less* caffeine than a full mug of filter coffee (around 95 mg). The picture flips once you order a double shot or a large milk drink with two or three shots.

## Cold brew

Cold brew is made by steeping coarse coffee in cold water for many hours, usually at a high coffee-to-water ratio. Some cafés serve it as a concentrate diluted with water or milk; others serve it nearly straight. That makes cold brew one of the most variable drinks. If your café publishes a value, use it. Otherwise 150–200 mg for a 350 ml serving is a reasonable starting estimate — and it may be higher.

## Tea

Tea leaves contain caffeine, but a cup of steeped tea usually contains less than a cup of coffee because less leaf is used and not all of the caffeine is extracted. Black tea typically has more than green tea, and longer or hotter steeping extracts more. **Matcha** is different: you drink the whole powdered leaf, so it can contain noticeably more caffeine than steeped green tea. Hong Kong-style milk tea is often brewed strong, so it may sit towards the higher end of the black-tea range.

## Soft drinks and energy drinks

Canned drinks are the easiest to estimate, because many brands list caffeine on the label or website. A standard 355 ml can of cola typically contains about 30–45 mg. A 250 ml energy drink typically contains about 80 mg, but larger cans and “extra strength” versions can contain 150 mg or more. In the European Union, drinks other than tea and coffee that contain more than 150 mg of caffeine per litre must be labelled “High caffeine content” and “Not recommended for children or pregnant or breast-feeding women”, with the amount per 100 ml.

## Decaf is not caffeine-free

Decaffeination removes most — not all — of the caffeine. A typical cup of decaf contains only a few milligrams, but laboratory testing of coffee-shop decaf has found up to about 15–18 mg in a large serving. That is fine for most people, but several decaf cups in the evening do add up, so it is worth logging them.

## How to estimate your own drinks

1. **Look for a published number.** Many chains and drink brands publish caffeine values per size. Use them over generic averages.
2. **Count shots.** For espresso drinks, multiply the number of shots by about 63 mg as a starting point.
3. **Scale for size.** For filter coffee, start from about 95 mg per 240 ml and scale by your cup size.
4. **Scale for strength at home.** If you use twice the usual amount of coffee, expect roughly twice the caffeine.
5. **Round up when unsure.** For sleep planning, overestimating is safer than underestimating.
