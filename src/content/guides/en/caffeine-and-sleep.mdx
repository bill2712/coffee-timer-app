Plenty of people say “coffee doesn’t affect my sleep”. Sometimes that is true. But sleep studies keep finding the same pattern: caffeine taken in the afternoon or evening often shortens and lightens sleep — and people frequently do not notice. This guide explains why, summarises what the research shows, and turns it into practical cutoff times.

## How caffeine keeps you awake

While you are awake, a substance called **adenosine** gradually builds up in the brain. Adenosine is one of the signals behind “sleep pressure”: the longer you have been awake, the more of it there is and the sleepier you feel. Caffeine works mainly by **blocking adenosine receptors**. The adenosine is still there, but its message is muffled, so you feel more alert than you otherwise would.

That is helpful at 9 a.m. At 11 p.m. it means the same thing: your brain receives a weaker “time to sleep” signal for as long as enough caffeine remains. Because caffeine leaves the body slowly — with a typical half-life of around 5 hours (see our [caffeine half-life guide](/en/guides/caffeine-half-life/)) — a coffee in the afternoon can still be partly active at bedtime.

## What the research shows

Several well-designed studies have measured sleep objectively after people took caffeine at different times.

**Six hours is not always enough.** In a 2013 study in the *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine*, healthy sleepers took 400 mg of caffeine (roughly four cups of filter coffee) either at bedtime, 3 hours before or 6 hours before bed. All three timings disturbed sleep compared with a placebo. Even at 6 hours, measured total sleep time fell by about an hour — yet participants largely did not report sleeping worse.

**The effect is consistent across studies.** A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis in *Sleep Medicine Reviews* pooled 24 studies. On average, caffeine reduced total sleep time by about **45 minutes** and sleep efficiency by about **7%**, took about **9 minutes** longer to fall asleep, and added about **12 minutes** of wakefulness during the night. Deep sleep was reduced too. Based on these data, the authors suggested that a typical coffee (about 107 mg per 250 ml) should be consumed **at least 8.8 hours before bedtime** to avoid losing sleep, and a large pre-workout dose (217.5 mg) at least 13.2 hours before.

**Dose matters a lot.** A 2025 randomised crossover trial by the same research group gave young men 100 mg or 400 mg of caffeine 4, 8 or 12 hours before bed. The **100 mg** dose caused no significant objective sleep disruption. The **400 mg** dose did: it lengthened the time to fall asleep when taken 4 hours before bed and reduced sleep efficiency when taken 8 hours before. Again, participants were not good at judging the effect themselves.

EFSA’s 2015 opinion on caffeine reached a similar practical conclusion: single doses of around 100 mg may affect sleep duration and patterns in some adults, particularly when consumed close to bedtime.

## Why you might not notice

A recurring finding is that caffeine changes sleep more than people perceive. You may fall asleep only a little later, wake briefly a few more times or spend less time in deep sleep without remembering it. The result shows up the next day as feeling less refreshed — which many people treat with more coffee. That loop is easy to miss, which is why a simple estimate of what is left in your body at bedtime can be useful.

## Practical cutoff times

There is no single cutoff that suits everyone, because it depends on **how much** you drink, **how fast** you clear caffeine and **how sensitive** you are. Two reasonable approaches:

### 1. The research-based rule of thumb

For an ordinary cup of coffee (around 100 mg), aim to finish it **about 8–9 hours before you plan to sleep**. For an 11 p.m. bedtime, that means around 2–3 p.m. For larger drinks — a big cold brew, a double-shot latte, an energy drink or a pre-workout — make the cutoff earlier, around 12–13 hours before bed, especially if your bedtime is early.

The [caffeine calculator](/en/caffeine-calculator/) estimates the latest time for one more 95 mg cup based on what you have already had, your bedtime and your half-life. With an empty day and an 11 p.m. bedtime, it gives these results:

Notice that the default 50 mg threshold gives **later** cutoffs than the research-based rule above, especially for fast metabolisers. That is intentional: 50 mg is a moderate general guideline, not a guarantee of good sleep. If you want to follow the cautious advice from the 2023 meta-analysis, lower your sleep threshold to around 25 mg — with an average half-life, the calculator’s cutoff then moves to early afternoon, close to the 8.8-hour figure.

These numbers also move earlier as soon as you have had other caffeine. With a 126 mg double espresso at 8 a.m. and a 95 mg coffee at 1 p.m., an average metaboliser already has about 40 mg left at 11 p.m., and the calculator would say one more cup does not fit under a 50 mg threshold.

## Tips for keeping caffeine away from bedtime

- **Move your caffeine earlier, not just lower.** The same total amount does less harm to sleep if it is front-loaded in the morning.
- **Switch to decaf or half-caf after lunch.** Decaf still contains a few milligrams, but far less than regular coffee.
- **Watch the size.** A large café drink can contain two or three times the caffeine of a home mug. Our [guide to caffeine in drinks](/en/guides/caffeine-in-drinks/) shows typical ranges.
- **Count hidden sources.** Black and green tea, cola, energy drinks, pre-workout powders, chocolate and some pain relievers contain caffeine.
- **Do not use caffeine to cover a short night.** Heavy afternoon caffeine after a poor night can shorten the next night’s sleep too. A short walk in daylight or a brief early-afternoon nap may help more.
- **Keep your routine consistent.** Regular sleep and wake times, morning light, and a dark, cool bedroom matter as much as caffeine timing.

## When to get help

If you regularly struggle to fall or stay asleep even after cutting caffeine, or if you feel your heart racing, anxiety or tremors after modest amounts, talk to a doctor. Persistent insomnia is common and treatable, and it is worth checking for other causes such as sleep apnoea, stress, medications or other health conditions.
